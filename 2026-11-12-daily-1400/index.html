Nvidia founder Jensen Huang resigns, stock falls 6.0%

An interim chief executive takes over the biggest chip company days before earnings. Oil reverses higher as tankers burn in the Strait of Hormuz, Brooklyn counts eight dead, and the 10-year Treasury yield trades over 5%.

By Josh Pilcher
Scroll

What's Moved

  • 1

    Nvidia has a new chief executive and no permanent one. Jensen Huang, who co-founded the chip company in 1993 and ran it for 33 years, resigned as president, chief executive and director on Thursday morning, effective at once. Colette Kress, the finance chief since 2013, takes over on an interim basis and keeps her old job, and director Harvey C. Jones becomes the first chair in company history. The shares closed the morning down sharply and stood at $163.30 early in the afternoon, after falling about 14% at the low. Earnings land on Tuesday, November 17. More: Reuters

    The Ukraine talks produced one agreement and one opening. After two days of American-brokered talks in Abu Dhabi, Russia and Ukraine agreed in principle on a large prisoner exchange and on nothing else: no ceasefire, no territory, no guarantees. On Thursday in the Kremlin, President Vladimir Putin agreed to let European representatives join the next round, according to an American envoy, before envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner left for Dublin to brief President Volodymyr Zelensky. More: Reuters

    Oil reversed. Brent futures had fallen in early trade, then rose 1.3% to $93.96 a barrel by 12:08 PM Eastern after President Donald Trump posted that the United States does not need a deal with Iran. Iran's military said four tankers were hit by explosions in the Strait of Hormuz, the waterway that carried nearly a fifth of the world's crude before the war; Reuters could not verify that account. More: Reuters

On Thursday the city's Department of Buildings said unsafe construction work at the property next door caused the collapse, and the mayor ordered inspections at about 100 other sites tied to the five companies linked to the project. More: USA TODAY

Abu Dhabi and Moscow

Prisoner swap agreed, ceasefire refused, talks move to Moscow

Russia and Ukraine are fighting a war that began with Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022. This week the United States brought both sides to Abu Dhabi for their first brokered meeting in months, and the only result was an agreement in principle on a large prisoner exchange. Russia would not discuss a ceasefire, territory, or the security guarantees Ukraine says it needs before any settlement, and it refused even a pause in attacks on the power grid.

On Thursday, American envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner carried the outcome to President Vladimir Putin in the Kremlin, then set out for Dublin to brief President Volodymyr Zelensky. While the delegations traveled, a Russian drone hit an apartment block in Kyiv, killing one woman and injuring five people.

Russian attacks that weekend knocked out electricity for over 600,000 people in the Dnipropetrovsk region and nearly 250,000 around Kharkiv, while negotiators were preparing to meet. Heat and light are the pressure point of the winter campaign, and the campaign does not pause for talks.

Khan Younis, Gaza

Strike on Nasser Hospital kills six, including its surgeon

An Israeli airstrike hit the surgery department of Nasser Hospital in Khan Younis, in southern Gaza, just after midnight on Thursday. Six people were killed, among them general surgeon Rizk Abu Shaira, and at least 28 were wounded. Nasser is one of the few hospitals still functioning in Gaza after years of war.

Israel's military said it targeted the commander of Hamas's Khan Younis Brigade and other militants inside the hospital. A ceasefire brokered by the United States has nominally held since October 2025, and strikes have continued through it.

Jakarta

Indonesia's vice president resigns over his school papers

Gibran Rakabuming Raka, Indonesia's 39-year-old vice president and the son of former president Joko Widodo, handed his resignation to parliament on Thursday. Two courts ruled in October that the Singapore school certificate he used to qualify for the 2024 election did not prove the secondary education Indonesian law requires for the office.

The resignation arrived a day after 120 lawmakers filed a petition to impeach him. President Prabowo Subianto must now nominate two candidates, and the national assembly chooses one by majority vote within 60 days of the vacancy.

Taiwan Strait

G7 warns China over waters east of Taiwan, Beijing objects

The foreign ministers of the G7 group of large democracies issued a joint statement on Wednesday night saying China's increasingly assertive actions in the waters east of Taiwan threaten regional stability and freedom of navigation. They restated their opposition to any one-sided change to the status quo by force or coercion.

China claims Taiwan as its own territory, and Taiwan's elected government rejects that claim. China's Foreign Ministry answered on Thursday that the statement interferes in China's internal affairs.

Madrid

Spain's first general strike in 14 years was about rent

Spain held a nationwide general strike on Wednesday, its first in 14 years, over housing costs, wages and the rising cost of living. Unions said more than 3 million people took part in protests across the country.

Police arrested 115 people, mainly during evening clashes in Barcelona and Madrid. The strike puts housing policy, and rents in particular, at the center of Spanish politics heading into winter.

Flatbush, Brooklyn

Eight dead in Brooklyn collapse, city blames the dig next door

A three-story apartment building at 1321 Flatbush Avenue, built in 1930 with four apartments over a storefront, fell into the open excavation pit beside it at about 4 PM on Wednesday. Eight people died: two children and six adults, including two construction workers. Eighteen people were injured, among them four firefighters, and one person remains in critical condition.

On Thursday the Department of Buildings said the collapse was caused by unsafe construction operations at the adjacent property, where excavation was under way. The site held approval for a three-story building; a nine-story plan had not been approved. More than 250 firefighters and rescue personnel are still searching the rubble, the fire chief says the work could take several days, and nobody yet knows how many people are missing. The city has also ordered the neighboring buildings at 1323, 1325 and 1327 Flatbush Avenue emptied.

Juneau, Alaska

Alaska is the last Senate race, and it is close

Democrat Mary Peltola leads Republican Senator Dan Sullivan by about 4,300 votes, 49.0% to 47.2%, with about 84% of ballots counted. Alaska counts its Senate vote by ranked choice, and that tabulation is still to come, so the race stays uncalled.

On Wednesday an Alaska judge refused the Peltola campaign's request for a list of rejected absentee ballots, ruling that the state elections division is too busy processing ballots to produce it. If Peltola holds on, the Democratic majority grows by one.

Washington

America's measles status, held since 2000, is under review

A health commission for the Americas is meeting in Washington this week, November 10 to 13, to decide whether the United States keeps its measles elimination status. The status, held since 2000, means the disease has not spread continuously inside the country for 12 months or more.

An outbreak that started in West Texas in January 2025 is still going, which is exactly the test the commission applies. Federal health officials counted about 5,200 confirmed cases in 2026 through November 5, the first year over 5,000 since 1991, and public health experts widely expect the status to be revoked.

Housing

Home sales fall to a 16-year low of 3.75 million

Sales of previously owned homes fell 5.5% in October to an annual rate of 3.75 million, the lowest since August 2010 and below the 3.85 million economists expected. September's rate was 3.99 million. High borrowing costs are doing the work: buyers who locked in cheap mortgages years ago have little reason to move and take on a far more expensive loan.

Jobs

Jobless claims rise to 200,000, a touch above forecasts

First-time claims for unemployment benefits rose by 9,000 to 200,000 in the week ended November 7, the Labor Department said on Thursday. Economists had expected 195,000. The level is still low by historical standards, which is why the Federal Reserve can talk about raising rates in December without pointing to a weakening job market.

Houston & Texas

Houston

Citgo earns $2.2 billion in one quarter, and still cannot be sold

Citgo Petroleum, the refining company headquartered in Houston and owned by Venezuela, reported third-quarter net income of $2.2 billion on Thursday, against $167 million in the same quarter last year, as strong refining margins lifted every part of the business. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization reached $3.0 billion, against $363 million a year earlier.

Its three refineries ran at record rates, processing about 904,000 barrels a day. Yet who owns Citgo is still undecided: a court-run sale approved a $5.9 billion bid last year from an affiliate of the hedge fund Elliott Investment Management, and the deal still waits for a green light from the US Treasury Department that has not come.

More: Reuters
Texas grid

Grid chief wants data centers cut earlier in an emergency

Pablo Vegas runs ERCOT, the organization that operates the Texas electric grid. Speaking in Houston on Wednesday, he said he will ask state lawmakers to let ERCOT order data centers to reduce power use at the first of its three emergency levels, or even before it, instead of at the second level as the rules stand today.

The request matters because of the line of customers behind it: about 474 GW of large power requests, most of them data centers, are waiting to connect to a grid whose all-time peak demand is 91.1 GW. A state report due December 10 will say how much of that line is backed by real projects.

Houston City Hall

Council members want the new ICE limits written into law

On November 6, Mayor John Whitmire ordered Houston police to contact federal immigration authorities, known as ICE, only when a person is already being jailed on new charges or other warrants and a database check shows an ICE warrant. Immigration status by itself is no longer enough to trigger a call, a longer stop, or a hold.

Three council members, Alejandra Salinas, Edward Pollard and Tiffany Thomas, now propose writing those limits into city law, with more detail in the police department's quarterly reports, so no future mayor can undo them with a signature. Whitmire opposes the additions, arguing they raise the city's risk under a Texas law that restricts local limits on immigration cooperation. City lawyers are reviewing the proposal, and no vote date is set.

Texas

Sheriffs face a December 1 deadline to sign ICE agreements

A Texas law called Senate Bill 8 requires every sheriff who runs a jail to sign a cooperation agreement with ICE, under a federal program known as 287(g), by December 1. By mid-September, 202 of the 232 offices covered by the law had signed. Harris County, which includes Houston, is among the holdouts.

The law says the state attorney general may sue a sheriff who misses the deadline. It does not say the attorney general must. From January, that choice belongs to the incoming attorney general, Nathan Johnson, a Democrat who voted against the law when he served in the state senate.

Markets

Nvidia

Nvidia plunges at the open, then claws back half the loss

The resignation of Jensen Huang took the stock down as much as 14% in the morning, to $148.80, before buyers stepped in. At 2:40 PM Eastern it stood at $163.30, down 6.0% on the day and about 32% below the record close of $239.24 set on October 6.

Analysts who cut the stock on Thursday blamed the empty chair, not the chips: Bernstein lowered its price target from $400 to $200, Baird from $500 to $200, and Oppenheimer withdrew its target altogether. President Trump, for his part, suggested former Apple chief Tim Cook for the job. The first real test is Tuesday, November 17, when the company reports earnings against its own guidance of $108.0 billion in revenue.

The rivals

Broadcom and AMD rise on Nvidia's bad day

Money leaving Nvidia went looking for the companies that compete with it. Broadcom rose more than 5% to lead the gainers in both the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq 100, and Advanced Micro Devices rose more than 4% by midday and about 6% by mid-afternoon, trading at $586.24.

The bet is simple: if the dominant seller of AI chips is distracted by a leadership search, some orders drift to the number two and number three sellers. Apple, meanwhile, touched an all-time high in morning trade, untouched by the chip turmoil.

Nebius

Nebius beats, then falls 20% on its spending plan

Nebius, a company that rents out AI computing power, reported sales up 511% to $981 million and a loss of 13 cents a share, far better than the 72 cent loss analysts expected. The stock still fell more than 20%, because the company raised its planned capital spending to about $27 billion for 2026 and $43 billion for 2027, well beyond what investors had penciled in.

Corteva drops 23% on weak sales in Brazil

Corteva, which sells seeds and crop chemicals to farmers worldwide, fell more than 23% after reporting third-quarter sales of $1.59 billion, down 9%, and cutting its full-year outlook. The company pointed to weak conditions in Brazil, one of the largest farm markets in the world.

Worth Knowing

Florida coast

The fastest ocean liner ever is now a reef

The SS United States, the ocean liner that set the transatlantic speed record on its first voyage in 1952 and still holds it, was deliberately sunk off the Florida coast on Wednesday. The ship will serve as the largest artificial reef in the world, a habitat for marine life and a site for divers.

The liner had been out of service for decades and passed through a long line of owners and rescue plans before the reef plan won. What was once the fastest way across the Atlantic is now a place for fish and divers.

Europe

Bomb threats hit about 7,000 schools, and none was real

About 6,300 schools in Italy and more than 700 in Scotland and Northern Ireland received emailed bomb threats on Wednesday, and more Italian schools were evacuated on Thursday. Police checks found no evidence of any real device or concrete danger.

Italy's prime minister, Giorgia Meloni, described the messages as a strategy meant to cause alarm. Investigators in several countries are tracing where the emails came from.
