Nvidia loses its founder, and a hot inflation print pushes yields through 5%

Stocks closed lower on Thursday as Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang is out, October inflation ran hot enough to put a December rate hike in play, and the search in Brooklyn ended with eight dead. Texas is building a new government, and the audit world got a rule proposal worth reading.

By Josh Pilcher

Markets

Business

Jensen Huang resigns as Nvidia chief executive, and Colette Kress takes over as interim

Nvidia, the chipmaker at the center of the artificial intelligence buildout and worth about $4 trillion, announced on Thursday morning that co-founder Jensen Huang, 63, had resigned as chief executive and as a board member, effective immediately. Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress stepped in as interim chief executive while the board searches for a permanent successor, and board member Harvey Jones became the first chairman in the company's history.

The exit followed reports of an affair with a former executive at a Singapore cloud company whom United States authorities concluded was a Chinese intelligence asset, which Beijing denies, and a second reported affair with a senior Nvidia executive who reported directly to him. Nvidia shares fell 4.3% on the day to close at $166.19, and they are down 11% on the week. Rival chipmaker Advanced Micro Devices rose about 6% as investors weighed who gains if Nvidia wobbles.

The economy

October inflation rose 0.4%, and a December rate hike is now the base case

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on Tuesday that consumer prices rose 0.4% in October, more than the 0.3% economists expected, while core prices, which strip out food and energy, rose 0.3% for the third straight month. This is the last inflation report the Federal Reserve, the central bank that sets short-term interest rates, will see before it meets on December 8 and 9.

Hours before the data landed, Fed Governor Christopher Waller said a core reading at that level should put a half-point hike on the table, not just the usual quarter point. Traders now treat a quarter-point rise, from the current range of 3.75% to 4.00%, as fully priced, with about a 14% chance of the bigger move. The yield on the 10-year Treasury note, the benchmark borrowing rate the whole economy prices off, climbed to about 5.0% at Thursday's close, and gold fell 3.0% to $3,882 an ounce.

Cisco raises its year outlook after revenue jumps 24% to $18.5 billion

Cisco, the networking equipment maker, reported after Thursday's close that revenue for its fiscal first quarter reached $18.5 billion, up 24% from a year earlier and ahead of the $18.1 billion analysts expected, as data center orders tied to artificial intelligence kept growing. It raised its full-year revenue forecast to a range of $74.8 billion to $75.8 billion.

Applied Materials posts record revenue of $10.4 billion, up 53%

Applied Materials, which sells the machines chip factories use to make advanced semiconductors, reported record quarterly revenue of $10.4 billion, up 53% from a year earlier, with adjusted earnings of $4.16 a share against $4.06 expected. It forecast about $11.1 billion of revenue for the current quarter, also ahead of expectations, which says the spending on chip capacity is not slowing even as its biggest customer's leadership turns over.

Moscow

American envoys reach Moscow after Ukraine talks end with a prisoner swap and no ceasefire

President Donald Trump's envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner arrived in Moscow on Thursday to meet Russian President Vladimir Putin. They came from Abu Dhabi, where two days of American-brokered talks among the United States, Russia and Ukraine ended on Wednesday with agreement on a large exchange of prisoners and no progress toward stopping the fighting in a war Russia launched in 2022.

The war itself did not pause for the diplomacy. Russian strikes on Ukraine's power grid left about 850,000 customers without electricity in two eastern regions, and a drone strike on a Kyiv apartment block killed one woman. After Putin, the envoys are due in Ireland to meet Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy.

Gaza

An Israeli strike on the largest hospital in southern Gaza kills six, including a surgeon

An Israeli airstrike early on Thursday hit a building at Nasser Hospital in Khan Younis, wrecking the surgery department of the largest hospital still operating in southern Gaza. Officials from Gaza's Civil Defence agency and the hospital said at least six people were killed, among them a surgeon, Dr. Rizk Abu Shaira, and about 20 people were wounded.

Israel's military said the target was a senior Hamas member and other militants it accused of using the hospital as a command center. Hamas is the armed group that governs Gaza and whose attack on Israel in 2023 started the current war.

Strait of Hormuz

Oman works a grounded oil tanker off its coast while Hormuz shipping stays blocked as a bargaining chip

Oman's national emergency committee met on Thursday about an oil tanker grounded off Al Qibliyah Island and about incidents in the waters off Musandam, the part of Oman that overlooks the Strait of Hormuz. The strait is the narrow waterway between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula that carried nearly a fifth of the world's crude oil before the war between the United States, Israel and Iran, now in its ninth month.

Oman did not name the tanker or say how it grounded. Oil leaking from tankers attacked in the strait has already fouled beaches in the Emirates and Oman, and whether ships can move through Hormuz at all remains one of the open questions in the talks aimed at ending that war.

Jakarta

Indonesia's vice president resigns after the courts void the school certificate he ran on

Gibran Rakabuming Raka, Indonesia's vice president and the 39-year-old son of former president Joko Widodo, handed his resignation to the national parliament on Thursday, a day after giving it to President Prabowo Subianto. Indonesian law requires a vice presidential candidate to have finished senior high school or an equivalent qualification.

The country's Supreme Court annulled the equivalency certificate Gibran used to qualify for the 2024 election, and 120 lawmakers had started a process that could have removed him anyway. Parliament now has up to 60 days to pick a replacement from two names the president submits, in Southeast Asia's largest economy.

New York

The Brooklyn search is over: eight dead and 18 injured in the Flatbush building collapse

A three-story apartment building at 1321 Flatbush Avenue in Brooklyn collapsed into the construction pit next door at about 4 p.m. on Wednesday, while crews were excavating the adjoining lot. Mayor Zohran Mamdani said on Thursday that two children and six adults were killed, two of the adults construction workers, and that 18 people were injured, one of them critically.

The Fire Department ended its search on Thursday afternoon and said no victims remained in the rubble, after more than 250 firefighters and medics worked the site. City records show the 1930 building had racked up 98 violations since 1972, and the city says work at the neighboring site had earlier gone ahead despite a stop-work order. Inspectors are now sweeping about 100 other sites tied to the same companies.

Washington

A gunman kills one man at a Washington gym and wounds four more, then kills himself

Shortly before 8 a.m. on Thursday, a man opened fire inside a VIDA Fitness gym in Logan Circle, a residential neighborhood about a mile north of the White House, killing the building's manager, James Stewart, 46. Police say the gunman then went to his own apartment in the same building, fired from above and wounded four more people, one critically, and set a fire before shooting himself after about an hour of negotiation.

Interim Police Chief Jeffery W. Carroll identified the gunman as Matt Wade Lambert, 55, and said he had been in a dispute with the building's homeowners association, the body that manages the shared property where he lived.

Health

The United States is likely to lose the measles-free status it has held for decades

A regional body of the World Health Organization, the United Nations health agency, opened a four-day meeting to decide whether the United States still counts as having eliminated measles. That status means the virus is not spreading continuously inside the country, as opposed to arriving in isolated cases from abroad.

The signs point to losing it. The country has recorded more than 5,400 measles cases this year, its worst outbreak since 1991, in a disease that a two-dose childhood vaccine prevents in the great majority of cases. The head of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention said the agency's response would not change either way.

Harris County

Harris County certifies its count: about 1.4 million voters and 50.5% turnout

Harris County, the county that contains Houston and the most populous in Texas, released its official final count on Tuesday for the November 3 election. About 1.4 million people voted, a turnout of 50.5%, counting mail ballots, early votes, Election Day ballots and provisional ballots.

The county went heavily for the Democrats who also won statewide. James Talarico, the Democrat elected to the United States Senate, took 65.6% of the Harris County vote, and Gina Hinojosa, the Democrat elected governor, took 63.6%. Those margins are the local shape of the statewide sweep that is now reshaping the government Josh lives under.

Houston

The Astros bring back Brad Ausmus, the catcher from their 2005 pennant team, as manager

The Houston Astros named Brad Ausmus as manager on Wednesday. Ausmus, 57, caught for Houston for 10 of his 18 seasons as a player, including the 2005 team that won the National League pennant, and he has since managed Detroit and the Los Angeles Angels and spent three seasons on the New York Yankees' coaching staff.

He replaces Joe Espada, whose contract was not renewed after three seasons ended with a playoff sweep by the Chicago White Sox. Ausmus is the 21st manager in Astros history and the first major hire by Randy Flores, the general manager appointed two weeks ago. He will be introduced on Friday at Daikin Park, the Astros' downtown stadium.

Texas

Texas's incoming Democratic leaders are putting Republicans on their transition teams

Democrats won every statewide office in Texas on November 3 and took control of the state House of Representatives for the first time in a generation. This week the winners started staffing the changeover, and they are not staffing it with Democrats only: Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa named a former Republican state representative, Ernest Bailes, as a co-chair of her transition team, and the incoming lieutenant governor and the presumptive House speaker have made similar cross-party appointments.

Senator-elect James Talarico has cautioned his own side against treating the result as permanent. Two years ago Donald Trump carried Texas by 14 points. The new officials take office in January.

Texas

Angry parents are on course to flip the board that sets what Texas schools teach

The State Board of Education writes Texas's curriculum standards and approves its textbooks. After last week's elections, Democrats who campaigned against the education policies of the outgoing Republican leadership, among them history teacher Michelle Palmer, are positioned to control that board for the first time in more than 30 years, with one close race still uncalled.

An August survey found 45% of Texans disapproved of the state's approach to public schools against 28% who approved. The ceiling on change is real: Republicans kept the state Senate, so the incoming governor's school agenda still needs votes from the other party.

Audit regulation

The audit regulator wants to retire the 135-day rule for comfort letters

The Public Company Accounting Oversight Board, the federal regulator that oversees the firms that audit public companies, has proposed changing AS 6101, its standard on comfort letters. A comfort letter is the document an auditor gives to the banks selling a company's securities, saying nothing in the financial statements has changed materially since they were issued.

Today that assurance runs on a fixed clock of 135 days. The proposal scraps the fixed number and lets the period run up to the company's next required filing deadline instead, which lines the audit rule up with the securities filing calendar and leaves room for a possible shift from quarterly to twice-a-year reporting. Anyone can comment until January 4, 2027.

Quality control

The SEC signs off on tougher quality control rules for audit firms

On November 2 the Securities and Exchange Commission, the markets regulator that oversees the audit board, formally approved amendments to QC 1000. That is the standard that governs the system every audit firm must run to police its own work: how it assigns people, reviews engagements and fixes defects before they reach investors.

More: SEC
Standard setting

FASB takes up goodwill write-downs, and a pension rule heads to its final draft

The Financial Accounting Standards Board, the private body that writes American accounting rules, put goodwill impairment testing on its November 11 meeting agenda. Goodwill is the premium recorded when one company buys another for more than its identifiable assets are worth, and the rules for when that premium must be written down have been argued over for years because the write-downs tend to arrive late and all at once.

A week earlier the board told its staff to draft a final standard on how pension accounting applies to market-return cash balance plans, a hybrid retirement plan design. Separately, the heads of the SEC, the audit board and FASB all addressed the same financial reporting conference this week, the annual circuit where the next year's reporting agenda gets set in public.
