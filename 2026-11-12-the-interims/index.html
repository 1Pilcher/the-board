The Interims

The founder is out, the winners are not seated, and the talks produced a promise instead of a ceasefire. Power this week did not move to the calendar. It moved to the people who can wait.

By Josh Pilcher · Thursday, November 12, 2026
Scroll

Santa Clara, California

The man is gone. The chair is empty.

Thirty-three years, one chief executive, no successor. On Thursday morning the most valuable chip company in the world opened with an interim in the job.

Photograph via Invezz. Jensen Huang keynoting Nvidia’s GTC conference. The roadmap, the order book and the stock price were set from that stage, in one voice. On Thursday the voice resigned.

What’s Moved

Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia’s president, chief executive and director, effective immediately. The board accepted the resignation on Wednesday, November 11. Colette Kress, chief financial officer since 2013, is interim chief executive and keeps the finance job. Harvey C. Jones, a director since 1993, is the first chair in the company’s history. The announcement gave no reason.

Nvidia fell more than 10% in premarket trading on Thursday (Reuters). The fall extends a slide that began weeks earlier: the stock closed at a record $239.24 on October 6 and at $173.73 on Wednesday, down 27.4% before the resignation was announced.

Abu Dhabi ended on Wednesday with an agreement in principle on a large prisoner exchange and nothing on a ceasefire, territory or guarantees. The numbers are not settled. US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Vladimir Putin in Moscow on Thursday, then left for Dublin to brief Volodymyr Zelensky.

Russia refused to discuss even an energy truce in Abu Dhabi. Strikes over the November 8 and 9 weekend left more than 850,000 customers without power: over 600,000 in Dnipropetrovsk, DTEK said, and nearly 250,000 in Kharkiv, local officials said.

Iran said four tankers were hit by explosions in the Strait of Hormuz, two of them supertankers on Wednesday night. Reuters could not verify the account. Brent slipped about 1% in Asian trading on Thursday, then reversed higher in the US morning.

Mississippi was called for Democrat Scott Colom over Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith, 49.2% to 48.7% on the Associated Press count, a margin of 4,669 votes out of 962,237. Democrats now hold at least 55 Senate seats in the Congress that convenes on January 3. Alaska is uncalled.

In Houston, Citgo reported a record third quarter: $2.2 billion of net income on refining margins, while the court-approved $5.9 billion sale of its parent still waits on a Treasury licence and an appeals court.

Verdict

The named leaders left the board this week and the board did not stop. Huang is gone from Nvidia with no successor named and no timetable for finding one. The election winners in Washington and Texas won majorities they cannot use until January. The negotiators in Abu Dhabi and Moscow produced a process, not a settlement.

What filled the space, in every case, was an interim: a finance chief holding two jobs, a first-ever chair running an open-ended search, a lame-duck Congress that owns December precisely because it is leaving, a Treasury that can hold a $5.9 billion sale shut by saying nothing, and sheriffs, judges and grid staff whose discretion decides what the deadlines everyone is watching actually do.

Dates did not take the leverage. The people who can ignore the dates did.

On Thursday morning in Santa Clara, the most valuable chip company in the world opened its doors with a chief executive who had been chief executive for less than a day, a chairman who had been chairman for less than a day, and a founder who was suddenly a shareholder and nothing else.

Colette Kress has run Nvidia’s money for thirteen years. She knows every number the company will report on Tuesday. What she does not have, what nobody at Nvidia has, is the other job Huang did. Around sixty executives reported to him directly. The product roadmap was announced in his keynotes. The argument that kept Chinese customers on American technology was made by him, in person, to a president who repeated it back as his own. The board’s statement thanked him and explained nothing.

That is the shape of the week, repeated in four capitals. The person is out. The institution is an interim. And the interim, it turns out, is where the power went.

Chapter One

The Empty Chair

A founder exit with no succession, priced in stages. What Tuesday can and cannot settle.

Start with the price, because the price is where the story is usually told wrong. Nvidia did not fall 10% on Thursday because a founder resigned on Thursday.

The stock set its record close, $239.24, on October 6. By Wednesday’s close at $173.73 it had already lost 27.4% of that value, in a selloff that hit the whole chip sector and reflected doubts about the return on artificial intelligence spending, the loss of the China market, and then, from late October, the scandal. The affair reporting cost the stock measurably on November 9 and 10, when Nvidia fell while the chip index rose. Thursday’s premarket fall, more than 10% by Reuters’ count, bundled the resignation with a downgrade cascade and with positioning ahead of earnings.

The downgrades deserve a precise reading, because they are the market’s actual verdict. Bernstein’s Stacy Rasgon cut his target from $400 to $200 and downgraded the stock, and said the reason was the leadership vacuum, not the business: Nvidia’s fundamentals, in his words as reported, remain clearly intact. Baird cut from $500 to $200. Oppenheimer withdrew its target entirely. Morgan Stanley kept its $300 target but removed Nvidia as its top pick. Bank of America held at $350. A target cut that blames the chair, not the chip, is a governance discount. Governance discounts do not close with one good quarter.

Which is why Tuesday matters in a specific way. Nvidia reports fiscal third-quarter results on November 17, against its own guidance of $108.0 billion in revenue, plus or minus 2%, a gross margin of 74.0%, and no data center compute revenue from China. The quarter being reported is Huang’s quarter, August through October. Kress will present it holding both of her jobs. What the market is listening for is not the backward number. It is the forward guidance, and who gives the succession answer, and whether the investigation is described as open or closed. An interim can beat a revenue guide. An interim cannot, by definition, settle who runs the company.

The rest of the Nvidia board is a Washington board now. Three House committee chairs asked Huang, before he resigned, to brief them behind closed doors on November 18, one day after earnings. The request was made to a chief executive. It now lands on a private citizen who holds about 3.6% of the company, roughly 870.6 million shares, and no office that obliges him to appear. Senators Warren and Coons have asked the administration to preserve Nvidia’s China export records. The lame-duck Congress is trying to attach binding chip export limits to the defense bill that must pass before January 3, which would move allocation decisions from the Commerce Department’s licensing desk into statute. Whoever the board eventually hires will inherit a China policy written, in part, by a Congress that will no longer exist when they take the job.

Underneath the governance fight, the physical business has not moved, and the record supports saying so plainly. No supplier has reported a change in allocation, packaging capacity or delivery schedules because Huang left. TSMC still makes the chips. The hyperscalers still spend: the four largest guided to roughly $720 billion to $740 billion of combined 2026 capital spending. CoreWeave, the most leveraged buyer in the chain, reported third-quarter revenue of $3.66 billion on Tuesday night, raised its full-year guide to $13.0 billion to $13.3 billion, and carries a backlog of $132.5 billion against about $46.9 billion of debt. Its quarterly interest expense, $846 million, is roughly four times its operating income. At a 10-year Treasury yield of 4.98% (November 10, the last trading day before the Veterans Day closure), every Federal Reserve hike raises the cost of the buildout that Nvidia’s order book depends on. The founder’s exit does not change that arithmetic. It changes who negotiates it.

One more actor benefits quietly. Chinese domestic chip makers rose on Thursday, and Chinese AI firms, per Reuters reporting, are rushing to secure Nvidia capacity abroad before Washington tightens remote access rules. Beijing does not need to win the argument about what moved through Southeast Asia. It needs the argument to continue. Every week of it is industrial policy for substitution, delivered free.

Chapter Two

The Grid Negotiates

Abu Dhabi produced the one deliverable that always closes. The war produced the rest.

Abu Dhabi was the fifth year of the same negotiation wearing a new venue. The pattern is stable enough to state plainly. Prisoner exchanges close. Nothing else does.

In Istanbul in May 2025 the two sides swapped 1,000 prisoners each, the largest exchange of the war, and agreed on nothing political. In Geneva in February 2026 they swapped 314 in total and agreed on nothing political. This week they agreed in principle to swap again, with Zelensky citing 1,000 a side and possibly 1,500, Kushner saying the final numbers would be settled in Moscow, and no number yet announced as final. Treating an unsettled number as a breakthrough is how this track has been misread for two years.

The substance did not move at all. The Russian delegation, led by Vladimir Medinsky, who led Russia’s delegation in the first meeting of the war in February 2022, had, by Ukrainian officials’ account, no mandate to answer questions on ceasefire or territory, and refused to discuss an energy truce. Putin, meeting the envoys in the Kremlin on Thursday, held to the line that settlement means the four annexed regions, including territory Russia does not hold, and that European troops in a postwar Ukraine would be targets. The envoys’ concrete Moscow errand was procedural: permission for Europeans to join the next round. Witkoff said on departure that a new meeting was set and Europeans would take part. No readout from any capital contains a concession on territory, a ceasefire, or guarantees.

Kyiv reads guarantee language against a specific document. In 1994 Ukraine gave up the world’s third largest nuclear arsenal for the Budapest Memorandum’s assurances: respect for borders, no force, consultation. The memorandum had no enforcement mechanism, and Russia violated it in 2014 and again in 2022. That is why the guarantee wording, not the ceasefire line, has stalled every round since the 28-point plan of November 2025 was cut down in Geneva. A promise from the same capitals, with the same absence of enforcement, is not a new offer. It is the old one restated.

While the delegations talked, the other negotiation continued on schedule. Russian strikes on November 8 and 9 knocked out power for more than 850,000 customers, over 600,000 in Dnipropetrovsk by DTEK’s count and nearly 250,000 in Kharkiv by local officials’ count, and hit substations across central regions during the talks themselves. That figure is a weekend figure, recycled through the week, and it should be read as the opening position of the winter campaign, not as Thursday’s news. The campaign is in its fifth winter. Its logic has not changed: heat and light are the pressure, and pressure rises as temperatures fall, regardless of who is sitting in Abu Dhabi.

The least reported lever of the week sits above the battlefield. Zelensky said in Dublin that Starshield, the military satellite service Ukraine uses to intercept drones, has faced a speed restriction, reported at 161 km/h, for several days, hampering interceptions. SpaceX told CNN there had been no change in service. Who imposed the restriction, if it exists, is disputed among the White House, the Pentagon and the company, each of which has given a different account this autumn. The dispute is the story. A capability Ukraine depends on can be throttled without a signature, a vote or an announcement, by an actor no one will name, in the middle of a negotiation the United States is mediating. That is interim power in its purest form: unowned, unaccountable, and decisive while it lasts.

Chapter Three

Two Prices for One Strait

The benchmark prices deliverable barrels. The strait prices transit. They can diverge for a long time.

Two supertankers were disabled in the Strait of Hormuz on Wednesday night, on a route Tehran calls unauthorized, and Iran said two more tankers were hit on Thursday. None of it is independently verified. Reuters said so in the same story that carried the claim.

And Brent, which settled at $92.76 on Wednesday, slipped about 1% in Asian trading and then reversed higher in the US morning. The market is not ignoring the strait. It is pricing two different things at once, and only one of them is the benchmark.

The paper price is pricing deliverable barrels. OPEC’s November report put Declaration of Cooperation output up 2.04 million barrels a day in October, to 41.23 million. Saudi Arabia is the swing inside that number, and Riyadh’s own reporting contains the tell: it told OPEC it produced 10.563 million barrels a day in October, while secondary sources measured 9.19 million. The gap, 1.37 million barrels a day, is mostly storage by Riyadh’s own footnote. Claimed capacity is negotiating leverage into the November 29 OPEC ministerial that sets 2027 baselines. Measured supply is what the market can buy. Both numbers are Saudi instruments.

The physical price has barely moved, and it is the honest one. UKMTO counted 170 outbound and 155 inbound transits in the week to November 6, about 65% below pre-conflict levels. War-risk insurance for Gulf transits was quoted at 7% to 9% of hull value in early November, against 0.25% before the war.

A choke point can lose its benchmark premium and keep its physical rent. That is where Hormuz is.

Tehran’s position hardened in public while the tankers burned. Parliament speaker Ghalibaf said the strait will never return to its prewar arrangements. Iran has sent seven conditions through Qatar and says it has had no formal US reply. The full text of the seven has not been published, and any verbatim list in circulation is a reconstruction. The demands named by Iranian officials include ending the war on all fronts, lifting the US naval blockade, lifting sanctions, releasing frozen assets and compensation. Qatar, the most exposed actor on this board, with roughly a fifth of global LNG supply and no export route that avoids the strait, has responded by buying insurance of a different kind: 7 million tonnes a year of future US LNG supply under deals signed in October, deliveries starting in 2030. That is a state pricing a strait it expects to stay broken for years, in its own procurement.

The blockade, meanwhile, is run by interims too. The US naval blockade, reimposed on July 14 after the June memorandum collapsed in three weeks, has redirected 147 vessels by CENTCOM’s count, and a new task force of about twenty people in Tampa, announced November 7, now works the land routes. Washington’s line is that the economic campaign is devastating enough that major combat is unnecessary. Tehran’s line is that nothing reopens until its conditions are met. Neither line has a date on it. The strait’s future will be set by whoever tires first, and tiring is not scheduled.

Chapter Four

December Belongs to the Departing

The Mississippi call completed the map. It changed nothing about December, and understanding why is the whole Washington story.

Colom’s win, by 4,669 votes on the AP count with state certification on December 3, gives Democrats at least 55 Senate seats, 53 Democrats plus two independents, with Alaska’s ranked-choice count still to come and Mary Peltola leading.

Fifty-five is a real majority with specific, limited powers. From January 3 it controls committees, subpoenas, the floor schedule and every nomination. It is five votes short of ending a filibuster and twelve short of overriding a veto or removing an impeached official. It is a blocking and investigating majority, not a governing one.

The Hegseth case shows the arithmetic in advance: the House voted 211 to 204 on Tuesday to send the impeachment articles to committee, ending them in this Congress, with eleven Democrats absent. Even refiled in January, removal would need 67 Senate votes that do not exist.

Until January 3, power sits with the Congress that lost. The outgoing Republican majority has announced a 54-day program: 23 judicial nominees and nearly 100 executive nominees pending as of November 10, the defense bill, and government funding that expires on December 11. Lifetime judicial appointments confirmed in December outlast every other actor in this report. The incoming majority’s only December instrument is refusal: most funding bills need 60 Senate votes, so Democrats can withhold consent and run the clock toward a Congress they control. Both sides understand the trade. One side is buying permanence, the other is selling time.

The Federal Reserve votes inside that window, blind. The FOMC meets December 8 and 9. October inflation, published November 10, rose 0.4% on the headline and 0.3% on core, the third straight 0.3% core print, and no annual rate exists because the government never collected the October 2025 base. Governor Waller had said, before the print, that he leaned toward a 25 basis point hike and that a core print at 0.3% should put 50 on the table. The print landed exactly on his line. Dallas Fed President Logan, speaking in Houston on November 10, called for at least two more hikes. Tuesday’s futures pricing put a December hike as close to certain in size, roughly 85% to 90% for 25 basis points and about 10% for 50. November’s CPI arrives December 10, the day after the decision, and the funding deadline lands December 11. The sequence is not a stack that one actor controls. It is three separate rooms deciding in the wrong order, which is why December’s outcome belongs to no one, and why the people with gavels today intend to use them before the rooms change hands.

The terrain under the hike is strong enough to permit it. Third-quarter growth was 3.4% annualized. October payrolls rose 165,000 with unemployment at 4.1%. Wednesday’s market, the last full session before Thursday’s shock, closed with the S&P 500 at 7,619, up 1.3%, the Nasdaq at 25,466, up 1.6%, and the chip index up 5.7%, on CoreWeave’s print and an Apple record close of $355.63. The bond market was closed for Veterans Day, so the rally carried no Treasury verdict. Rates set the ceiling for the next two quarters. Whether the AI buildout hits that ceiling gradually or all at once is now, in part, a question about one company’s chairmanship.

Chapter Five

Houston

A record quarter with an asterisk, and a city converting an election into law one signature at a time.

Houston’s biggest company story this week is a number with an asterisk, and the asterisk is the story. Citgo reported third-quarter net income of $2.2 billion, up from $167 million a year earlier, with EBITDA of $3.0 billion, throughput of 904,000 barrels a day and crude utilization of 103% across its three refineries.

Those are the company’s figures, published Thursday, in the middle of a court-run sale of its parent company. Read them as a bid document as well as an earnings report and they make complete sense.

The sale has been approved since November 2025: $5.9 billion from Amber Energy, an Elliott affiliate, to settle Venezuela-linked creditor claims. It has not closed. It needs a Treasury licence that has not been issued and a Third Circuit ruling that has not landed, and Amber faces an April deadline to keep its offer alive. Against that fixed price, Citgo now guides to $7.2 billion to $7.5 billion of EBITDA for the full year and year-end cash of $6.7 billion to $7.0 billion.

The asset is on pace to earn more than its purchase price in a single year. Every quarter Treasury says nothing, the gap widens, the appellants’ undervaluation argument strengthens, and the leverage moves from the buyer holding a court order to the seller and creditors holding time. Houston’s stake is the headquarters and the Corpus Christi refinery. The decision sits in Washington and Philadelphia, on no published schedule.

City Hall spent the week converting an election into policy, and the fight is over durability, not direction. Proposition A, the Freedom Act, passed November 3 with 71.5%, about 413,000 votes in favor. It makes misdemeanor marijuana possession under four ounces HPD’s lowest enforcement priority and bans no-knock warrants. It legalizes nothing. Possession remains a state crime, and the measure binds only Houston police inside the city limits. It is also not yet in force: Council must formally adopt it, and the mayor controls the adoption pace and the training that follows. Whitmire’s position is that HPD already works this way. The organizers’ own numbers say arrests for misdemeanor possession averaged about 440 a year under Whitmire, against about 35 a year in 2021 to 2023, which is precisely why they wanted it in the charter, beyond any mayor’s reach.

The same mechanism ran in the opposite direction three days after the election. On November 6 Whitmire signed Executive Order 1-74, limiting HPD contact with ICE to cases where a person is already being arrested on a jailable offense and matches an administrative warrant. He said he acted after consulting the incoming statewide officials, who signaled the state’s sanctuary-city law is not their priority. In April, under threat of losing about $110 million in state funding, the city had moved the other way. Three council members, Salinas, Pollard and Thomas, now want the order written into city code, with expanded reporting, so a future mayor cannot reverse it with a signature. Whitmire opposes the additions. The argument is not about ICE. It is about whether November’s result gets stored in an executive order, which is one man’s property, or in law, which is everyone’s problem.

The Port keeps its own ledger, indifferent to the politics. Port Houston moved 376,419 TEUs in September, up 11% year over year, with loaded imports up 16%. Descartes data for October, imports only, showed Houston posting the largest monthly gain of any top-ten US port, up 26.7% against September, off a low base. Volume is not the port’s constraint. Delay is: average transit delays across major ports rose to 6.3 days in October. The channel sells reliability, and reliability is the number to watch.

The load is coming

Five times the grid, on paper.

Texas has 474 GW of large-load requests in line for a grid that has never carried more than 91.1 GW. December 10 publishes how much of that line is real.

Photograph via IBM. Rows of computing hardware, the load behind the Texas queue. The machines are real. The question the audit answers is how many of the requests behind them are.

Chapter Six

Texas

The sweep that does not control the Senate, and the deadlines that belong to the outgoing.

Texas is the interim problem drawn at state scale. Democrats won every statewide office, the US Senate seat, and the Texas House. Republicans kept the Texas Senate, 19 to 12.

Legislators and most statewide officers, including the incoming attorney general, take office when the legislature convenes on January 12, 2027. Governor-elect Hinojosa and Lieutenant Governor-elect Goodwin are not sworn in until January 19. In that first week, Dan Patrick still holds the gavel over a Senate that adopts its own rules by simple majority, 16 of 31, and the lieutenant governor’s real powers, committee assignments, bill referrals, control of the floor, live in those rules, not in the constitution. Republican senators met the day after the election to discuss reorganizing those rules (Quorum Report). Vikki Goodwin, who beat Patrick 51.7% to 45.6%, answered with a bipartisan transition team that includes former Republican senators, and with public restraint. She needs four Republican defections to stop a rules rewrite. The most important vote in Texas this winter happens before the woman it is about is sworn in, presided over by the man she beat.

The immigration deadline works the same way, one level down. Senate Bill 8 requires sheriffs who run jails to enter 287(g) agreements with ICE by December 1. As of mid-September, 202 of 232 required offices had one. Harris County, Dallas and Travis are among the holdouts. Goodwin said on November 9 that she wants the state out of these agreements. She cannot do anything about it. She holds no office, repeal would need the Republican Senate she does not control, and the December 1 deadline will be administered by the outgoing attorney general. What decides the question is a discretion: from January, the attorney general who may sue a noncompliant sheriff is Nathan Johnson, a Democrat who voted against the law. The statute says the attorney general may bring an action. May is the most powerful word in Texas government this winter. Sheriffs who stall past January are betting on it, openly.

Energy is where the state’s paper meets its physics. ERCOT’s president, Pablo Vegas, told a BloombergNEF audience in Houston on Wednesday that he will ask the legislature to let ERCOT order data centers to cut power earlier in an emergency, before any emergency is declared, rather than at the second of three alert levels as today. The ask lands in a divided legislature and needs Governor-elect Hinojosa’s signature. Behind it sits the queue: about 474 GW of large-load requests, roughly 90% of them data centers, against a record peak of 91.1 GW set on July 22. The Public Utility Commission’s rule, effective October 8, already prices a queue position at $50,000 per MW of posted security, $50 million for a 1 GW campus, posted before any study. ERCOT’s verification report is due to the commission on December 10, the commission takes up exceptions on December 17, and the audit rests on developer self-reporting: 433 of 465 loads answered, and the recurring gaps were site control, equipment orders and financial security actually posted. December 10 does not decide Texas power. It publishes who was bluffing. The decisions follow in the February capital plans, under a governor who has promised a rate freeze in a deregulated market that gives her no single rate to freeze, and a data center moratorium that Abbott, in substance, already imposed by directive in August. She inherits his pause. What she does with it is January’s question.

Chapter Seven

Two Rival Reads

Two coherent ways to read the same week. Each names the evidence that would settle it.

Read One

The interims consolidate

Placeholders, given time, become the settlement. Intel’s interim finance chief, Bob Swan, became permanent in 2019 and ran the company for two years. A lame-duck Congress that confirms judges in December has made the most durable decision available to anyone this winter. An attorney general who simply never files the SB 8 suit has repealed the law in practice while it sits on the books. On this read, Kress or a quiet insider is still running Nvidia next summer, the prisoner channel is the whole Ukraine policy, and Texas government through 2027 is the accumulated sum of decisions its elected winners never got to make.

Decide by: No permanent Nvidia chief executive named by the November 17 call or the weeks after it. No December SB 8 filings. A prisoner exchange executed with no dated next political round.

Read Two

The deadlines bite anyway

Interims hold power only until a forcing event prices their delay. November 17 forces Kress to speak for the company’s future in public, with a stock down more than a quarter from its record. December 11 forces a funding answer from a Congress that needs 60 votes it must borrow from the other party. April forces Amber Energy to close, extend or lose a $5.9 billion deal that gets worse for it every quarter. On this read, the interims are not rulers, they are tenants, and the calendar is the landlord.

Decide by: A permanent succession timetable announced with earnings. A December funding deal struck on the incoming majority’s terms. An OFAC licence or a Third Circuit ruling that starts the Citgo clock in public.

The deciding evidence arrives in that order: earnings language first, December filings second, the Citgo docket third. Until then, both reads are live, and the honest position is that the placeholders hold the board and the deadlines hold the placeholders.

Watchlist

What Changes the Analysis

Five developments, in the order they land. Each one moves a specific claim in this report.

  • 1November 17, Nvidia earnings. The number matters less than the voice: guidance given by an interim holding two jobs, any succession timetable, and any statement on the investigation’s status. A clean beat with no succession answer confirms the governance discount.
  • 2November 18, the House briefing requested of Huang. Who appears, Kress, counsel, or no one, tells Washington whether the company intends to cooperate with a Congress that is about to change hands. A subpoena would end the interim era’s ambiguity.
  • 3The prisoner exchange. A final number, executed, at or above the 1,000 a side of May 2025, plus a dated next round with Europeans inside the room, is the only version that counts as movement. Anything less is the pattern repeating.
  • 4December in Washington, in sequence: the FOMC on December 8 and 9, November CPI on December 10, funding on December 11. A 50 basis point hike, or a funding lapse that runs into the new Congress, changes every other calculation in this report.
  • 5Texas, December 1 and December 10. Whether the outgoing attorney general files any SB 8 suit in December, and how much of the 474 GW queue survives ERCOT’s verification filing. Both are counts. Both will be published. Count them.
