10-Year Yield Closes Above 5% as the Fed Chorus Turns to a December Hike

Stocks gave back their midday gains as Nvidia slid in the first session after Jensen Huang's resignation. Europe won a promised seat at the next Ukraine talks, another tanker blew up in Hormuz, and Senate Democrats got a leadership fight. Houston gets an ICE ordinance fight, a record Citgo quarter and a grid request with real teeth.

New York · The close

Stocks give back midday gains and close lower across the board

Every major index was higher at midday yesterday and every one of them finished lower. The S&P 500 closed at 7,592, the Nasdaq Composite at 25,366 and the Dow at 52,297, each down 0.4% on the day, as investors weighed a slide in Nvidia after its chief executive resigned and a broad rise in Treasury yields. Breadth was weak underneath the big names: nine of the eleven S&P sectors fell, materials dropped 1.5%, and energy was the only sector with a gain worth the name, up 1.5% on dearer oil.

Washington · The economy

Fed officials line up behind a December hike, and a half point is on the table

The Federal Reserve, the US central bank, sets its policy rate at meetings of its Federal Open Market Committee, and the next one falls on December 8 and 9. Consumer prices for October, published on November 10, rose 0.4% on the month, with core prices, which strip out food and energy, up 0.3%, both hotter than economists expected. Governor Christopher Waller had already said he would back a quarter-point hike in December even if inflation cooled, and that a half-point move should be considered if core prices rose 0.3% or more. They did.

The chorus has since widened. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan expects two more quarter-point hikes, possibly delivered faster this winter. New York Fed President John Williams said yesterday evening that more rate increases are likely and that policy is not yet in the right place to bring inflation back to the Fed's 2% target. Futures markets price roughly a 90% chance of a quarter-point move in December, which would take the target range to 4.00% to 4.25%, and about a one-in-six chance of a half point. The next test is the October producer price report, out this morning, where economists expected wholesale prices to rise 0.4% on the month.

Santa Clara · Business

Nvidia closes down 4.3% at $166.19 as its chip rivals climb

Nvidia, the chipmaker at the center of the artificial intelligence build-out, announced yesterday that Jensen Huang, who co-founded the company in 1993 and ran it for 33 years, had resigned as president, chief executive and a board member, effective immediately. The announcement gave no reason. Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress, in the finance job since 2013, takes over as interim chief executive while keeping the finance role, and director Harvey C. Jones becomes the board's first-ever chair while it searches inside and outside the company for a permanent successor.

The stock opened at $151, against a close of $173.73 the day before, then recovered part of the fall to close down 4.3%. Its rivals took the other side of the trade: Advanced Micro Devices gained 5.9% and Broadcom rose 5.5%. The resignation came four days after the board opened an independent review of reports that Huang had been targeted by a foreign intelligence operation; the board has said it has no reason to believe national security or the company's secrets were compromised, and yesterday's announcement did not say whether the review has concluded. Senate Banking Committee Chair Tim Scott added a Washington thread yesterday evening, sending letters asking Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to testify before his panel next month. The first business test lands quickly: Nvidia reports quarterly results on Tuesday, November 17, with Kress chairing her first earnings call as interim chief.

Redmond · Business

Microsoft 365 outage runs into a second day, with no time given for a full fix

A failure in Microsoft's workplace software, the email, calendar and meeting tools that much of corporate America runs on, began yesterday afternoon and was still not fixed this morning. The fault sits in Exchange Online, the system behind Outlook email: servers brought back into service keep going unresponsive once traffic reaches them, and a patch to a routing and authentication component is only partly relieving the strain. Microsoft says it is not aware of any malicious activity behind the outage and that capacity has not yet returned to normal, though it reported improvement in North America, Europe and the Asia Pacific region this morning.

The reporting service Downdetector logged about 130,000 problem reports for Outlook at yesterday's peak, with reports coming from 74 countries and territories. Microsoft has extended its email timeout window to 48 hours so that delayed messages are not lost, and the incident is being tracked under two references, EX1550543 and MO1550821, still listed as a service degradation with no promised restoration time.

World

Moscow and Dublin · Ukraine talks

Putin restates his full terms, and Europe is promised a seat at the next round

American envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Russian President Vladimir Putin in the Kremlin yesterday for about two hours, in the latest round of US-led talks aimed at ending Russia's war in Ukraine. Putin's position did not move: he called Russia's claimed border, which includes all four Ukrainian regions Moscow says it has annexed, the only clear demarcation line, called foreign troops in a post-war Ukraine absolutely unacceptable, and restated the demands on NATO that Russia first tabled in December 2021.

The format did move. The envoys flew on to Ireland and briefed Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky for more than an hour. Zelensky said Europe will be directly represented at the next round of talks, after months in which Moscow worked to keep Europeans away from the table, and that the prisoner exchange now being arranged will be the largest of the war. Kushner said Putin is willing to give European participation a try; Putin's aide Yuri Ushakov gave a more guarded reply and did not confirm the point. No date or place for the next round has been announced, the exchange is expected around the end of November, and Putin and President Trump are expected to speak about Ukraine at the APEC summit in Shenzhen, China, on November 18 and 19.

Strait of Hormuz · Iran

An LNG tanker hits a mine in Hormuz, and Trump pulls Vance off the Iran file

Iran's Revolutionary Guard navy said this morning that a tanker carrying liquefied natural gas struck a naval mine and suffered an explosion in the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway at the mouth of the Gulf through which a large share of the world's oil and gas passes, while using a route the Guard calls unauthorized and dangerous. It named no vessel and gave no casualty or pollution information. The Guard says four tankers in total were hit by explosions on Wednesday and yesterday, two of them supertankers disabled on Wednesday, and it also said its forces destroyed an American early-warning radar in Bahrain, home of the US Navy's 5th Fleet. The Associated Press reports that the radar claim could not be verified and that US Central Command had not responded.

The diplomacy moved the other way. President Trump posted yesterday that the United States does not need a deal with Iran, and pulled Vice President JD Vance, who had been the administration's channel toward talks with Tehran, off the file and onto fraud investigations in Democrat-run jurisdictions. An American naval blockade of Iranian ports remains in effect.

Khan Younis · Gaza

Strike on the surgical wing of Nasser Hospital kills six, including a surgeon

An Israeli strike early yesterday hit the surgical wing of Nasser Hospital in Khan Younis, the largest hospital in southern Gaza and one of the few medical facilities still functioning there. Gaza's Health Ministry said six people were killed, among them general surgeon Rizk Abu Shaira, and 20 were wounded. Israel's military said it had struck a Hamas commander and other militants at the site; the Associated Press notes it provided no evidence for that claim. The hospital's director said the hospital received no warning.

The World Health Organization says it has documented more than 1,000 attacks on health care in Gaza over three years, and Nasser Hospital itself has now been struck seven times during the war.

Gulf of Finland · The Baltic

Finland confirms a Ukrainian strike on a Russian warship in the Baltic

Ukraine's Security Service says its forces struck the Naro-Fominsk, a Russian Baltic Fleet corvette armed with Kalibr cruise missiles, during Wednesday's raid on the port of Ust-Luga in Russia's northwest. Finland's defence forces confirmed that they detected a strike on a Russian warship near the Russian-held island of Gogland, about 40 kilometers off the Finnish city of Kotka: Finnish sensors recorded flashes and smoke, and the ship, which had been escorting the shadow-fleet tankers that carry Russia's sanctioned oil, turned back toward St Petersburg. Damage and casualties are unconfirmed, and Russia has not commented. Ukrainian attacks on Russian warships have until now been a Black Sea story; this one happened in international waters between two NATO states.

The region's governments are tightening their own arrangements in parallel. Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway and Sweden agreed in Oslo to start drafting a common defence agreement that would let their forces move and operate across each other's borders more easily, and said they are on track to triple their ammunition production capacity by 2030.

Washington · The Senate

Van Hollen challenges Schumer for Senate majority leader

Senator Chris Van Hollen of Maryland announced yesterday that he will run for Senate majority leader, challenging Chuck Schumer of New York, who has led the Senate Democratic caucus since 2017. The contest exists because Democrats won a majority in the Senate, and the House as well, in this month's midterm elections, so the winner of this internal vote will control which bills reach the Senate floor when the new Congress convenes in January. Van Hollen said voters had called for a change from a status quo that is not working for working people. Senator Bernie Sanders of Vermont is backing him, and Van Hollen claims the support of more than ten senators. Schumer answered that he has the votes to stay on. At least six incoming senators-elect have said they will not back him. The caucus decides by secret ballot in December, and a simple majority of its members wins.

Topeka · Kansas

Kansas calls its governor's race for Democrat Cindy Holscher, 50.3% to 49.7%

The Associated Press called the Kansas governor's race yesterday evening, two days after the vote. Cindy Holscher, a Democratic state senator, defeated Ty Masterson, the Republican president of the state Senate, by 50.3% to 49.7%. She succeeds Laura Kelly, the term-limited Democratic governor, and keeps the office in Democratic hands in a state that voted for President Trump by 16 points in 2024. Holscher campaigned on halting the construction of new data centers in the state, a pledge aimed at farming communities wary of industrial development on their land.

Brooklyn collapse toll reaches eight, and the inquiry widens next door

Part of a three-story building at 1321 Flatbush Avenue in Brooklyn fell into a neighboring excavation on Wednesday afternoon. White House warns incoming Democrats that their investigations could cross into criminality

The White House is putting next year's oversight fight in writing before it starts. Washington · Immigration

ICE reports record arrests and deportations for fiscal 2026

The Department of Homeland Security has released its figures for fiscal 2026, the government year that ended on September 30. Houston · City Hall

Three council members move to put Houston's ICE contact limits into city law

Three Houston City Council members, Alejandra Salinas, Edward Pollard and Tiffany Thomas, are proposing an ordinance that would write Mayor John Whitmire's new limits on police contact with federal immigration authorities into the city code, so that no future mayor could reverse them with a signature. The limits come from Whitmire's executive order of November 6, which directs Houston police to contact Immigration and Customs Enforcement only when a person is already going to jail on new charges or other warrants and a national database check turns up an ICE administrative warrant. Under the order, being subject to immigration enforcement, by itself, is not enough for an officer to contact ICE, extend a stop or take someone into custody: a driver cited at a traffic stop and released goes on their way even if a warrant appears.

The sponsors filed the proposal under Proposition A, the charter provision that lets three or more council members place an item on the council agenda. The city legal department reviews it first, and no date for a council vote has been announced. Whitmire's office says the mayor stands by his own order but believes the added provisions would put Houston at greater risk of violating Senate Bill 4, the Texas law that restricts local policies limiting cooperation with federal immigration authorities.

Houston · Energy

Citgo's record quarter: the profit is real, and so is the wait for a new owner

$2.2B

Third-quarter net income at Citgo, a company record

$5.9B

The court-approved bid for Citgo's parent, still waiting on a Treasury green light

Citgo Petroleum, the Venezuela-owned refiner headquartered in Houston, earned $2.2 billion in the third quarter, up from $167 million in the same quarter a year earlier, as strong refining margins met record running rates. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization came to $3 billion, against $363 million a year earlier, and its three refineries, in Louisiana, Texas and Illinois, processed 904,000 barrels a day, up 10% from the second quarter. The company raised its full-year earnings guidance to between $7.2 billion and $7.5 billion. None of it settles who owns the place: the sale of its parent company to an affiliate of Elliott Investment Management, approved by a court last year, still cannot be executed without the US Treasury's permission.

Texas · The grid

ERCOT's chief wants power to order data center cuts one emergency level earlier

The Electric Reliability Council of Texas, the operator of the Texas power grid, grades a supply emergency in three levels. Under today's rules, data centers, the vast computing warehouses whose power demand is remaking the state's grid, can only be asked to cut their consumption once an emergency reaches level 2. ERCOT's chief executive, Pablo Vegas, speaking on Wednesday at an energy summit in Houston, said he will ask state lawmakers to let the operator require those cuts at level 1, or even earlier, on the argument that ordering large customers down takes time and cannot wait until an emergency is already deep. A 2025 Texas law, Senate Bill 6, already obliges large electricity users to cut consumption when given a day's warning, but ERCOT has never directed a data center to scale back under it. No bill has been filed yet, and the request lands in a live fight over the industry's growth: the governor paused new data center grid connections in August, and ERCOT's audit of nearly 10 gigawatts of smaller data center projects is due on December 10.

Texas · Starbase

SpaceX puts $24 billion on an off-grid AI campus at Starbase

SpaceX, Elon Musk's rocket company, announced yesterday that it plans to invest more than $74 billion in two artificial intelligence supercomputing campuses, and the smaller of the two is in Texas. About $24 billion would go to Starbase, the company's base at the southern tip of the state, on a 444-acre tract near the Port of Brownsville, with about $50 billion more planned for a site in Cochise County, Arizona. Together the campuses target at least 10 gigawatts of computing capacity in 2027.

The Texas design is a statement about the grid: rather than join the years-long queue for a connection from ERCOT, the Texas campus would make its own power behind the meter, from a dedicated plant of 35 natural gas turbines fed by a proposed eight-mile pipeline. Starbase's city commissioners hold a public hearing and a vote on a property tax abatement for the project on December 9. The land itself is a recent maneuver: Brownsville, the neighboring city, gave up the parcel in August, shortly before adopting a 180-day moratorium on data centers, and Starbase took it in during September.

Washington · The standard setters

Atkins tells the FASB and the PCAOB to track the market, starting with private credit

Paul Atkins, chairman of the Securities and Exchange Commission, used a speech yesterday to the Corporate Financial Reporting Insights conference, an annual gathering of senior finance executives, to set out what he wants from the two boards the SEC oversees: the Financial Accounting Standards Board, which writes US accounting rules, and the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board, which oversees the audits of public companies. Both, he said, must respond to a changing market with discipline, weighing costs against benefits and keeping their work on what is material to investors.

His clearest applause went to a FASB research project called Current Trends and Emerging Issues, which watches developments such as data infrastructure investment, non-traditional lending and the debate over semiannual reporting, and which has already produced a project on private credit disclosure that now sits on the board's technical agenda, the list from which new standards come. On the audit side, he pointed to the PCAOB's first-ever request for public comment on its standard-setting agenda and to the quality control amendments the SEC approved earlier this month, and he reminded both boards that they are funded largely by issuers, through the accounting support fee, and should be judged on standards and audit quality rather than on their own growth.

Washington · Audit quality

SEC approves a softer QC 1000, effective December 15

QC 1000 is the PCAOB's standard for the quality control systems every audit firm must run over its own work. In an order dated November 2, the SEC approved the board's amendments easing it, and they take effect on December 15. The biggest change drops the requirement that a firm which issued more than 100 audit reports in the prior year build an External QC Function, an outside body to assess the firm's own evaluation of its controls. The amendments also scrap the design-only rule, under which a firm had to build a quality control framework before it had audited a single public company. The four largest firms, KPMG, PwC, Deloitte and EY, supported the changes in comment letters. The CFA Institute's Sandra Peters argued the other side, calling the external function the clearest structural safeguard investors have and proposing that it be kept for firms above a 500-audit threshold instead.

Norwalk · FASB

FASB takes up goodwill impairment, and a pensions standard heads to drafting

Goodwill is the premium a buyer books when it acquires a company for more than its identifiable net assets are worth, and testing whether that premium is still worth its carrying value is one of the most judgment-heavy exercises in accounting. The FASB had targeted improvements to goodwill impairment testing on the agenda of its November 11 meeting. A week earlier, at its November 4 meeting, the board directed its staff to draft a final standard on applying Topic 715, the pension standard, to market-return cash balance plans, a hybrid pension design whose benefits track market returns.

More: EY
Norwalk · Revenue

Comments close November 23 on a revenue proposal about payments made for customers

Topic 606, the revenue standard, treats money a company pays its customer as a reduction of revenue unless the payment buys a distinct good or service. A proposed update, out for comment until November 23, clarifies the harder cases outside a direct distribution chain: a payment made on a customer's behalf, or to a customer's customer. Under the proposal, a marketplace platform's credit to a buyer counts as consideration payable to the platform's customer, the seller, when the seller was promised the credit or reasonably expects it from custom or marketing. The FASB expects the clarification to track current practice in most cases and to cut the diversity that has grown up around it.

    Today is the doomsday date. A 1960 paper in the journal Science, by Heinz von Foerster, Patricia Mora and Lawrence Amiot, projected that world population would approach infinity today if growth kept its pre-1960 pace. It was written as a warning, not a prophecy. The model tracked reality until about 1993 and then failed as growth slowed, and the global birth rate now runs at a bit under half its 1960 level. A two-day conference in Vienna is marking the date.
