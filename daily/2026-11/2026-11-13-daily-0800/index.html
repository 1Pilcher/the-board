New York · The close

Stocks give back midday gains and close lower across the board

The S&P 500 closed at 7,592, the Nasdaq Composite at 25,366 and the Dow at 52,297, each down 0.4% after giving back midday gains, as investors weighed a slide in Nvidia after its chief executive resigned and a broad rise in Treasury yields. Breadth was weak underneath: nine of the eleven S&P sectors fell, materials dropped 1.5%, and energy alone gained, up 1.5% on dearer oil.

Washington · The economy

Fed officials line up behind a December hike, and a half point is on the table

The Federal Reserve sets its policy rate at Federal Open Market Committee meetings, next on December 8 and 9. October consumer prices, published November 10, rose 0.4% on the month, and core prices, which strip out food and energy, rose 0.3%, both hotter than economists expected. Governor Christopher Waller had already said he would back a quarter-point hike in December even if inflation cooled, and that a half point should be considered if core rose 0.3% or more. It did.

Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan expects two more quarter-point hikes, possibly faster this winter. New York Fed President John Williams said yesterday evening that more increases are likely and policy is not yet in the right place to bring inflation back to 2%. Futures price a roughly 90% chance of a quarter-point move in December, to a target range of 4.00% to 4.25%, and about one-in-six odds of a half point. The next test is October producer prices, out this morning; economists expected a 0.4% monthly rise.

Santa Clara · Business

Nvidia closes down 4.3% at $166.19 as its chip rivals climb

Nvidia announced yesterday that Jensen Huang, who co-founded the company in 1993 and ran it for 33 years, had resigned as president, chief executive and board member, effective immediately. The announcement gave no reason. Colette Kress, chief financial officer since 2013, takes over as interim chief executive while keeping the finance role. Director Harvey C. Jones becomes the board's first-ever chair, and the board is searching inside and out for a permanent successor.

The stock opened at $151, against $173.73 the day before, and recovered part of the fall to close at $166.19. Advanced Micro Devices gained 5.9% and Broadcom 5.5%. The resignation came four days after the board opened an independent review of reports that a foreign intelligence operation had targeted Huang; the board says it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised, and the announcement did not say whether the review has concluded. Senate Banking Chair Tim Scott added a Washington thread, asking Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to testify next month. Quarterly results come Tuesday, November 17, Kress's first earnings call as interim chief.

Redmond · Business

Microsoft 365 outage runs into a second day, with no time given for a full fix

A failure in Microsoft's workplace software began yesterday afternoon and was still unfixed this morning. The fault sits in Exchange Online, the system behind Outlook email: servers restored keep going unresponsive under traffic, and a patch to a routing and authentication component is only partly relieving the strain. Microsoft says it knows of no malicious activity behind the outage, and capacity has not returned to normal, though it reported improvement in North America, Europe and Asia Pacific.

Downdetector logged about 130,000 problem reports for Outlook at yesterday's peak, from 74 countries and territories. Microsoft has extended its email timeout window to 48 hours so delayed messages are not lost. The incident runs under two references, EX1550543 and MO1550821, still listed as a service degradation.

World

Moscow and Dublin · Ukraine talks

Putin restates his full terms, and Europe is promised a seat at the next round

American envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Russian President Vladimir Putin in the Kremlin yesterday, in the latest US-led talks to end the war. Putin's position did not move: he called Russia's claimed border, including all four Ukrainian regions Moscow says it has annexed, the only clear demarcation line, called foreign troops in a post-war Ukraine absolutely unacceptable, and restated the NATO demands Russia first tabled in December 2021.

The format did move. The envoys flew on to Ireland and briefed Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky for more than an hour. Zelensky said Europe will be directly represented at the next round, after months of Moscow keeping Europeans away from the table, and that the prisoner exchange being arranged will be the largest of the war. Kushner said Putin is willing to give European participation a try; Putin's aide Yuri Ushakov replied more guardedly and did not confirm it. No date or place for the next round has been set; the exchange is expected around the end of November.

Strait of Hormuz · Iran

An LNG tanker hits a mine in Hormuz, and Trump pulls Vance off the Iran file

Iran's Revolutionary Guard navy said this morning that an LNG tanker struck a naval mine and exploded in the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which a large share of the world's oil and gas passes, on a route the Guard calls unauthorized and dangerous. It named no vessel and gave no casualty or pollution information. The Guard says four tankers were hit on Wednesday and yesterday, two of them supertankers disabled on Wednesday, and that its forces destroyed an American early-warning radar in Bahrain, home of the US Navy's 5th Fleet. The Associated Press could not verify the radar claim, and US Central Command had not responded.

President Trump posted yesterday that the United States does not need a deal with Iran, and pulled Vice President JD Vance, his channel toward talks with Tehran, off the file and onto fraud investigations in Democrat-run jurisdictions. An American naval blockade of Iranian ports remains in effect.

Khan Younis · Gaza

Strike on the surgical wing of Nasser Hospital kills six, including a surgeon

An Israeli strike early yesterday hit the surgical wing of Nasser Hospital in Khan Younis, the largest hospital in southern Gaza and one of the few still functioning there. Gaza's Health Ministry said six people were killed, among them general surgeon Rizk Abu Shaira, and 20 wounded. Israel's military said it struck a Hamas commander and other militants at the site; the Associated Press notes it provided no evidence. The hospital's director said it received no warning.

The World Health Organization has documented more than 1,000 attacks on health care in Gaza over three years. Nasser Hospital has now been struck seven times during the war.

Gulf of Finland · The Baltic

Finland confirms a Ukrainian strike on a Russian warship in the Baltic

Ukraine's Security Service says its forces struck the Naro-Fominsk, a Russian Baltic Fleet corvette armed with Kalibr cruise missiles, during Wednesday's raid on Ust-Luga port. Finland's defence forces confirmed detecting a strike on a Russian warship near the island of Gogland, about 40 kilometers off Kotka: Finnish sensors recorded flashes and smoke, and the ship, then escorting shadow-fleet tankers carrying Russia's sanctioned oil, turned back toward St Petersburg. Damage and casualties are unconfirmed; Russia has not commented. Ukrainian strikes on Russian warships have until now been a Black Sea story; this one came in international waters between two NATO states.

Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway and Sweden agreed in Oslo to draft a common defence agreement letting their forces operate across each other's borders more easily, and said they are on track to triple ammunition production capacity by 2030.

Washington · The Senate

Van Hollen challenges Schumer for Senate majority leader

Senator Chris Van Hollen of Maryland announced yesterday he will run for Senate majority leader, challenging Chuck Schumer, who has led the Democratic caucus since 2017. The contest exists because Democrats won the Senate and the House in this month's midterms: the winner will control the floor when the new Congress convenes in January. Van Hollen said voters had called for a change from a status quo that is not working for working people. Bernie Sanders backs him, and Van Hollen claims more than ten senators. Schumer answered that he has the votes. At least six incoming senators-elect say they will not back him. The caucus decides by secret ballot in December; a simple majority wins.

Houston · City Hall

Three council members move to put Houston's ICE contact limits into city law

Three Houston City Council members, Alejandra Salinas, Edward Pollard and Tiffany Thomas, propose putting Mayor John Whitmire's new limits on police contact with federal immigration authorities into city law, where no future mayor could reverse them with a signature. The limits come from Whitmire's November 6 executive order, which directs Houston police to contact ICE only when someone is already going to jail on new charges or warrants and a database check turns up an ICE administrative warrant. Being subject to immigration enforcement alone is not enough for an officer to contact ICE, extend a stop or take someone into custody: a driver cited at a traffic stop and released goes on their way even if a warrant appears.

The sponsors filed under Proposition A, the charter provision letting three or more council members place an item on the agenda. The city legal department reviews it first; no council vote date has been announced. Whitmire's office says the mayor stands by his order but believes the added provisions would put Houston at greater risk of violating Senate Bill 4, the Texas law restricting local limits on cooperation with federal immigration authorities.

Third-quarter net income at Citgo, a company record

Citgo Petroleum, the Venezuela-owned refiner headquartered in Houston, earned $2.2 billion in the third quarter, up from $167 million a year earlier, as strong refining margins met record running rates. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization came to $3 billion, against $363 million a year earlier, and its three refineries processed 904,000 barrels a day, up 10% from the second quarter. Texas · The grid

ERCOT's chief wants power to order data center cuts one emergency level earlier

The Electric Reliability Council of Texas, operator of the Texas grid, grades a supply emergency in three levels. Under today's rules, data centers can only be asked to cut consumption once an emergency reaches level 2. ERCOT's chief executive, Pablo Vegas, speaking Wednesday in Houston, said he will ask lawmakers to let ERCOT require cuts at level 1, or even earlier: ordering large customers down takes time, and it cannot wait until an emergency is deep. A 2025 law, Senate Bill 6, already obliges large users to cut on a day's warning, but ERCOT has never directed a data center to scale back under it. No bill has been filed. The governor paused new data center grid connections in August, and ERCOT's audit of nearly 10 gigawatts of smaller projects is due December 10.

Texas · Starbase

SpaceX puts $24 billion on an off-grid AI campus at Starbase

SpaceX announced yesterday it plans to invest more than $74 billion in two artificial intelligence supercomputing campuses, the smaller of them in Texas. About $24 billion would go to Starbase, its base at the state's southern tip, on a 444-acre tract near the Port of Brownsville, with about $50 billion more for Cochise County, Arizona. Together the campuses target at least 10 gigawatts of capacity in 2027.

Rather than join the years-long queue for an ERCOT connection, the Texas campus would make its own power behind the meter, from a dedicated plant of 35 natural gas turbines fed by a proposed eight-mile pipeline. Starbase's commissioners hold a public hearing and vote on a property tax abatement on December 9. The land is a recent maneuver: Brownsville gave up the parcel in August, shortly before adopting a 180-day moratorium on data centers, and Starbase took it in during September.

Washington · The standard setters

Atkins tells the FASB and the PCAOB to track the market, starting with private credit

Paul Atkins, chairman of the Securities and Exchange Commission, used a speech yesterday to the Corporate Financial Reporting Insights conference to set out what he wants from the two boards the SEC oversees: the Financial Accounting Standards Board, which writes US accounting rules, and the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board, which oversees public company audits. Both, he said, must respond to a changing market with discipline, weighing costs against benefits and keeping their work on what is material to investors.

His clearest applause went to a FASB research project, Current Trends and Emerging Issues, which watches data infrastructure investment, non-traditional lending and the debate over semiannual reporting, and has already produced a private credit disclosure project now on the board's technical agenda. On the audit side, he pointed to the PCAOB's first-ever request for public comment on its standard-setting agenda and to the quality control amendments the SEC approved earlier this month, and reminded both boards they are funded largely by issuers, through the accounting support fee, and should be judged on standards and audit quality, not their own growth.

Washington · Audit quality

SEC approves a softer QC 1000, effective December 15

QC 1000 is the PCAOB's standard for the quality control systems every audit firm must run. The amendments were approved in an SEC order dated November 2. The biggest change drops the requirement that firms issuing more than 100 audit reports in the prior year build an External QC Function, an outside body to assess their own evaluation. The amendments also scrap the design-only rule, under which a firm had to build a quality control framework before auditing a single public company. The four largest firms, KPMG, PwC, Deloitte and EY, supported the changes in comment letters. The CFA Institute's Sandra Peters argued the other side, calling the external function the clearest structural safeguard investors have and proposing it be kept above a 500-audit threshold.

Norwalk · FASB

FASB takes up goodwill impairment, and a pensions standard heads to drafting

Goodwill is the premium a buyer books when it acquires a company for more than its identifiable net assets are worth, and testing whether that premium is still worth its carrying value is among the most judgment-heavy exercises in accounting. Targeted improvements to goodwill impairment testing were on the FASB's November 11 agenda. A week earlier, on November 4, the board directed staff to draft a final standard applying Topic 715, the pension standard, to market-return cash balance plans, whose benefits track market returns.

More: EY
Norwalk · Revenue

Comments close November 23 on a revenue proposal about payments made for customers

Topic 606, the revenue standard, treats money a company pays its customer as a reduction of revenue unless the payment buys a distinct good or service. A proposed update clarifies the harder cases outside a direct distribution chain: a payment made on a customer's behalf, or to a customer's customer. Under it, a marketplace platform's credit to a buyer counts as consideration payable to the platform's customer, the seller, when the seller was promised the credit or reasonably expects it from custom or marketing. The FASB expects the clarification to track current practice and cut the diversity around it.
