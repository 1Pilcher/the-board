The Study

The Gospel of Mark

The earliest Gospel, read slowly and honestly: who wrote it, when, what in it can be checked, and why it still sets the terms for everything written after.

Scroll

In Brief

This study reads the Bible the slow way: one book at a time, with the history left in and the hard questions left open. It begins with Mark, the earliest Gospel we have, and the shortest.

Mark was written within living memory of the crucifixion, probably around the year 70. It is urgent, plain, and strange, and it ends, in its earliest form, at an empty tomb, with no risen Jesus on stage at all.

Part One

The Text

What Mark actually says, before anyone explains it.

Mark opens without a birth story, without shepherds or wise men. His first line is a title for everything that follows: “The beginning of the good news of Jesus Christ, the Son of God.” Then John is baptizing in the wilderness, and Jesus walks into the water.

Everything in Mark moves at that pace. His favourite word is “immediately,” used more than forty times, and scenes follow one another like breaths. A man is healed; immediately a crowd gathers; immediately the trouble starts.

The Greek is rough, the sentences plain, the details oddly specific: green grass, two hundred denarii, a cushion in the boat. Scholars have long noticed that Mark reads less like literature composed at leisure and more like testimony written down in a hurry.

Part Two

The Backstory

Who wrote this book, when, and out of what world.

Nobody signed this book. The title “according to Mark” was added later, when churches held several Gospels and needed labels for the scrolls. The text itself never names its author, and never claims to be an eyewitness.

An early tradition, recorded by Papias around the year 130, says Mark was Peter’s interpreter, writing down what Peter preached. That may be true. It cannot be checked, and honest readers hold it the way scholars do: as a possibility, not a foundation.

The Gospel was most likely written around the year 70, as Rome destroyed Jerusalem and burned the Temple. Its first readers were frightened people asking whether God had abandoned them. Mark’s answer is a story, told fast, about a Messiah who suffers.

Part Three

What Is Historical

The claims of the book, graded against the evidence.

What can be checked, checked plainly: Jesus was baptized by John, gathered followers in Galilee, and was crucified under Pontius Pilate. Those points rest on more than one source, including writers with no love for the movement.

Part Four

What It Meant Then

What the first hearers heard in these words.

“Good news” was not a religious word first. It was the language of empire: the good news of a victory, of an emperor’s birth or accession. Mark’s first line takes Caesar’s word and gives it to a crucified Jew. His first hearers would have heard the provocation.

Sample text. A full session stays with the first audience: what they feared, what they expected a Messiah to be, and why this book disappointed those expectations on purpose.

Part Five

The Pattern

The shape underneath the story, and why it lasts.

Underneath the history sits a shape anyone can recognize. Mark’s Jesus is misunderstood by everyone closest to him, abandoned at the moment of greatest need, and vindicated only after the worst has happened. The disciples fail in this book more honestly than in any other.

Sample text. A full session draws the pattern out slowly: what this story trains a reader to expect from the world, and what it asks of them when the worst arrives.

Part Six

The Impact

What this book set in motion in the world.

Because Mark was first, his choices became Christianity’s defaults. Matthew and Luke used his book as their skeleton, keeping most of it and building on it. The plain, urgent, suffering Messiah of Mark is the ground floor under every later portrait, including John’s.

Sample text. A full session follows the consequences outward: how this one short book shaped two thousand years of art, ethics, and argument about what a good life costs.
