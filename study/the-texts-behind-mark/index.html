The Study

The Texts Behind Mark

The older scriptures Mark’s first readers already knew by heart: Daniel’s Son of Man, Isaiah’s suffering servant, the psalm that scripts the cross, and Abraham’s bound son.

Scroll

In Brief

What scriptures did the first Christians mean when they said Jesus died and rose “in accordance with the scriptures”? Where did Mark get the Son of Man, the ransom, the cry from the cross?

This session goes back before the Gospel, to the library its first readers already carried. Nine passages, written across roughly six hundred years: a voice in the wilderness, a father and son on a mountain, a human figure coming with clouds, the dead waking in the dust, a servant crushed for others, a psalm that begins in abandonment, and a king told to sit at God’s right hand.

Session 1 ended at a tomb, in silence, with a message nobody in the story was brave enough to repeat. This session asks what the people who did repeat it had to stand on.

The answer matters for the question this whole study turns on. The resurrection was not argued from nothing. It was argued from these texts, and the argument only works, or fails, on what these texts actually say and what their first readers already believed.

Part One

The Text

The passages themselves, in the order the Gospel and the first preaching reach for them.

Mark’s Gospel opens by quoting scripture before it tells a single event. So this session opens the same way: the texts first, in the open, in the order the Gospel, and then the first preaching after it, leans on them. Explanations wait their turn.

The first words of Mark are a citation. He attributes it to Isaiah alone. It is actually two prophets woven together, Malachi’s messenger and Isaiah’s voice, and the weaving itself is a first lesson in how these texts were handled: by heart, by theme, without apology.

Part Two

The Backstory

Who wrote these texts, when, and out of what crises.

No single author stands behind this session, and no single century. The nine passages were written across roughly six hundred years, by people who did not know each other’s names, in worlds that kept ending.

Hosea is the oldest voice here. He prophesied in the northern kingdom of Israel in the eighth century BC, in the decades before Assyria destroyed that kingdom in 722 BC. His “third day” was spoken to a nation already coming apart.

Genesis 22 belongs to the Abraham cycle, stories carried and shaped over centuries before they reached their present form. The binding of Isaac, the Akedah, as Jewish tradition calls it, is set on a mountain in the land of Moriah.

A later biblical writer identifies Moriah with the Temple Mount in Jerusalem. That is how the story came to live at the center of the sanctuary’s own geography.

Isaiah’s book is really three books bound as one. Chapters 40 to 55, the source of both the wilderness voice and the suffering servant, come from the exile in Babylon in the sixth century BC, two hundred years after the Isaiah of Jerusalem.

The writer never names himself. He names the Persian king Cyrus as God’s anointed, and Cyrus took Babylon in 539 BC, which dates the voice in the wilderness to the eve of the exiles’ return home.

The Psalms are a hymnbook assembled over centuries, songs for the Temple and for private grief, gathered long after the events they are attached to. The headings that call Psalms 16, 22, and 110 “of David” are later editorial labels, not signatures.

Psalm 110 began life, most scholars think, as a song for a king in Jerusalem, sung to him, about him, by his own court.

Daniel is the youngest book in the session by far. Its court tales may be older, but the visions, including the Son of Man and the resurrection, reached their final form in the 160s BC, during the persecution of Antiochus IV, a Seleucid king who outlawed Jewish practice and desecrated the Temple.

The book is set in Babylon in the sixth century BC. It was written in Judea some four centuries later, in a crisis its first readers were living through.

By the time of Jesus, all of it was already ancient, and already scripture. In caves near the Dead Sea, a community copied these books by hand, and their copies survived: the Great Isaiah Scroll, written around 125 BC, holds the whole of Isaiah, the suffering servant included.

Every book in this session was found among those scrolls. Mark’s readers did not receive these texts as suggestions. They received them as the word of God, memorized, sung, and argued over.

One more fact of the backstory matters for everything that follows. The first Christians read these books in Greek. The Septuagint, the Greek translation made in Egypt across the third and second centuries BC, was the Bible of the early church, and Mark’s quotations often follow its forms.

Where the Greek and the Hebrew differ, and they differ at some of this session’s most argued-over lines, the difference itself becomes part of the story.

Part Three

What Is Historical

What can be checked, what is contested, and what cannot be reached.

This part grades the session’s claims the way the study grades everything. Some of what follows is corroborated. Some is honestly contested. Some sits beyond the reach of checking, and the session will say so rather than pretend otherwise.

Start with what can be dated, because the dating here is unusually solid. Daniel’s visions retell history with precision down to the persecution of the 160s BC, and then, at the end of chapter 11, predict a death for Antiochus that did not happen as described. He died in Persia, not “between the sea and the glorious holy mountain.” A prophecy accurate until 164 BC and wrong immediately after is strong evidence of when it was written. The resurrection of Daniel 12 is a second-century BC idea, not a sixth-century one.

The exile behind Isaiah 40 to 55 is likewise corroborated in outline by Babylonian records and by the Cyrus Cylinder, the Persian king’s own inscription. The return happened. The highway in the wilderness was, for its first hearers, a road home.

Abraham and Isaac stand on different ground. No evidence outside the Bible reaches them, and the honest grade for the Akedah as an event is beyond checking. It is a story, told with a master’s economy, about a command and a knife stopped at the last moment.

What can be checked is the world behind it. Child sacrifice existed at Israel’s edges; the king of Moab offers his son on a wall in 2 Kings 3, and Micah 6:7 asks, as a real question, “Shall I give my firstborn for my transgression?” The story lives in a world where the question could be asked.

Now the idea this study most needs to date: resurrection itself. For most of the Hebrew Bible, the dead do not rise. They go down to Sheol, a shadowed place of silence, and God is praised by the living or not at all. Job despairs of any return. Ecclesiastes says the dead know nothing.

Daniel 12:2 is the first unambiguous break with that world: many who sleep in the dust shall awake, some to everlasting life, some to contempt.

Isaiah 26:19, “your dead shall live,” may be earlier. Scholars contest whether it promises individual resurrection or the revival of the nation pictured as a body rising. Daniel leaves no such doubt.

The servant’s identity is contested, and the contest is ancient. Isaiah’s own book names the servant as Israel in the chapters around the songs: “You, Israel, my servant” in chapter 41, and again in chapters 44 and 49. The songs themselves never name him.

Jewish reading, from the medieval commentators back through Origen’s Jewish interlocutors in the third century AD, has generally heard the nation in the servant’s suffering: Israel crushed among the nations, and then vindicated. Christian reading has heard an individual, and finally one individual.

The text underdetermines the answer, and both readings are honestly held by people who know the Hebrew. What can be said flatly is narrower: no surviving text from before Christianity reads Isaiah 53 as the prediction of a dying and rising messiah.

The Son of Man is contested in a different way. Daniel’s own interpretation, inside the same chapter, gives the human figure’s kingdom to “the people of the holy ones of the Most High.” That makes the figure read like a symbol for faithful Israel, set against the beast empires.

Later, the Similitudes of Enoch take the figure up as an individual, a hidden judge and king. But the Similitudes are the one part of Enoch missing from the Dead Sea Scrolls, and their date is genuinely unsettled, anywhere from the first century BC to the first century AD.

Whether a defined “Son of Man” figure existed for Mark to inherit, or whether Mark built the title’s weight himself out of Daniel, is an open scholarly question.

Psalm 16 needs the same honesty. In its own world it is a song of trust from a man who expects rescue in this life: do not give me up to Sheol means do not let me die yet. Nothing in the psalm speaks of a corpse returning.

The resurrection argument in Acts 2 works from the Greek translation’s wording, “corruption” rather than “the Pit,” and from the observation that David died and his tomb was known in Jerusalem, so the promise must belong to someone else.

That is an argument made from a translation, in Greek, about a Hebrew poem. Readers should see the machinery, because the machinery is the point: the earliest resurrection preaching was exegesis, an argument from texts, conducted in the open.

Psalm 22’s most famous detail is a translation problem too. The line about hands and feet is damaged or obscure in the Hebrew; the Greek translators rendered it “they dug my hands and feet,” and a copy of the psalm among the Dead Sea Scrolls supports a verb like the Greek’s. The New Revised Standard Version, cautiously, prints “they bound my hands and feet.”

Mark, in any case, does not quote that line. He quotes the psalm’s first line and stages its lots and its head-wagging. The pierced hands belong to John’s Gospel, not to this one.

Mark also quotes scripture the way his world did, loosely and from memory. He attributes a two-prophet composite to Isaiah alone. He quotes Zechariah 13:7 on the way from the Last Supper, “I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered,” without naming Zechariah. His Bible was a carried library, not a checked concordance, and the session should not grade him by standards his century did not have.

That leaves the contest at the center of this session, and it deserves both sides at full strength.

The case that the library was already loaded runs like this. By the first century, prophecy was being read as a coded timetable of the present, as the commentaries from Qumran show, using the very method Daniel itself models. Resurrection was live belief, held by Pharisees and by the martyrs’ tradition, not invented by Christians. Psalm 110 was royal currency, and Christians made it messianic currency within a generation. Isaiah’s servant was already being read messianically inside Judaism itself: the Targum, the Aramaic rendering used in synagogues, opens the last song with “my servant the Messiah,” even as it moves the suffering away from him onto Israel’s enemies. The first Christians inherited texts that were already heavy with expectation, and they read Jesus through them.

The case that the synthesis is Christian runs like this. Ingredients are not a recipe. No pre-Christian source combines the servant’s suffering, Daniel’s resurrection, and the Son of Man into one figure who dies and rises. A crucified messiah was, by the era’s own categories, a contradiction: messiahs were not executed by the empire they were meant to overthrow. The combination appears exactly when it is needed, after the crucifixion, among people who had staked everything on a man Rome killed, and who went back to the library to find out whether the story could still be true. On this reading, the texts did not predict the faith. The faith, under unbearable pressure, reread the texts, and the rereading was an act of genius.

The honest holder of this reading still owes one answer, though: rereading alone does not explain why the rereading produced resurrection claims, rather than an honored martyr and a waiting hope. Something pushed the reading where it went.

The session’s verdict is the sentence both sides can be graded against. The ingredients are demonstrably pre-Christian: every text in this session predates Jesus, was scripture before he was born, and was read with messianic and resurrection hopes by people who were not Christians.

The recipe, the combination of all of them in one crucified and risen man, is not attested before the Christians made it.

What neither side has yet explained, and what the next sessions take up, is the push: why the rereading, if that is what it was, took the shape of resurrection.

Part Four

What It Meant Then

What the first hearers heard in these words.

Daniel was read first by people being killed for keeping the law. Antiochus had made circumcision, Sabbath, and dietary practice capital matters, and the book’s own heroes are teachers, the wise, who fall “to refine and purify” while they wait for the end.

To those readers, Daniel 12 was not a doctrine. It was justice. If the faithful die for obedience and the collaborators prosper and die in their beds, then either God raises the faithful or the universe is a fraud. Resurrection enters the Bible as the answer to martyrdom.

The tradition shows the belief in action. In 2 Maccabees 7, a mother watches her seven sons tortured to death for refusing pork, and the brothers die saying Daniel’s promise aloud: the King of the universe will raise them to an everlasting renewal of life.

Their mother exhorts them with the same hope in her own words: the Creator who formed them will in his mercy give them life and breath again.

Whatever the chapter’s own date, and the book reached its form a generation or two after the persecution, it preserves how Daniel’s new idea worked on the ground while the persecution was still remembered at first or second hand.

Isaiah’s wilderness voice was heard first by exiles deciding whether to go home. The highway through the desert is the road from Babylon to Jerusalem, a new exodus, with God himself coming at the head of the column.

When Mark opens with it, he is telling his readers that what happened with John the Baptist belongs in that category: an exodus-scale event, a going-home of the whole world.

The servant’s first hearers were the same exiles, asking the exile’s question: what was our suffering for? The song answers that the servant’s suffering was not meaningless waste. It bore something for others.

A community that had been crushed could hear its own humiliation described as chosen, fruitful, and finally vindicated. The hearing itself was a kind of survival.

Psalm 110 was heard in a palace. It is a court song in which the singer addresses the king as “my lord” and reports God’s oracle to him: sit at my right hand. For centuries it promised the Jerusalem king a priesthood older than Aaron’s line and a reign without end.

The puzzle Jesus poses in Mark 12, how the messiah can be David’s son if David calls him lord, only works because his hearers knew the psalm as David’s own words about a greater king to come.

Psalm 16 was heard at the edge of a sickbed or a battlefield. Its joy is this-worldly: the boundary lines have fallen in pleasant places, the cup is full, the body rests secure. It is the prayer of a man who expects to be kept alive, and means to enjoy it.

Genesis 22 was heard on the mountain it names. Moriah became the Temple Mount, and the story was read where animals were offered daily, a standing declaration that this God, unlike the gods around Israel, finally provides the offering himself and stays the father’s hand.

The story does not explain itself. Its first hearers left it where it leaves us: with the boy walking back down the mountain beside his father, and Isaac saying nothing at all.

And Hosea’s third day was heard as a proverb before it was ever heard as a prediction: after two days, on the third, means in a little while, soon, the way rain follows the season. Keep that plain sense in mind. It is the ground everything later was built on.

Part Five

The Pattern

The shapes underneath the texts, and why they hold.

A discipline first, because this part needs one. What follows is not evidence, and it is not graded like Part Three. It is about reception: why claims built from these texts could be heard at all by the people who first heard them. Patterns are readings. This one is offered as the strongest available, no more.

Strip the session’s nine texts down and they reduce, honestly, to two shapes and one movement. The draft of a pattern that pretends to five separate shapes would be tidier and less true.

The first shape is the sufferer vindicated. It appears three times in the session, in three registers. Isaiah’s servant is crushed and then allotted a portion with the great. Psalm 22’s sufferer is abandoned, mocked, and counted among the dead, and the same psalm ends with him praising God in the congregation. Abraham’s son is bound on the altar and then walks down the mountain alive.

Three tellings, one movement of the heart: the one who suffers in God’s service is not finished at the point of suffering. The story does not end where the pain is.

Psalm 22 adds a mechanism the other tellings lack. In a culture that sang the psalms by heart, a first line invoked the whole song, the way a familiar opening carries its ending with it. This psalm ends in praise. A hearer formed by the singing, hearing its first line from the cross, hears the ending inside the abandonment. The two readings of that cry have been argued ever since, because the mechanism carries both.

The second shape is the human one set against the beasts. Daniel’s empires are animals, devouring and trampling. What God gives the everlasting kingdom to looks like a person. That contrast gave the first readers a standing way to judge power: the beast is what empire does to people, and the human figure is what rule is supposed to look like.

It also gave them a way to locate Rome without naming it, and every later reader a way to locate their own.

The movement underneath both shapes is down and then up. Daniel’s sleepers wake from the dust. Hosea’s torn nation is bound up on the third day. Psalm 16’s singer stands at Sheol’s edge and expects God’s hand to hold. The texts differ in almost everything else. They agree on the direction.

None of this proves anything about Jesus, and the session should be clear about that. What it explains is the hearing. By the first century these shapes were not ideas people evaluated. They were the furniture of worship: sung in the psalms, read in the synagogue, told to children, carried the way a language is carried.

A crucified messiah contradicted the shapes in one way, an executed king, and fitted them in another, a sufferer whose story was not finished at the cross. Which of those two hearings won, and why, is not a question the patterns can answer. It is the question the resurrection claim itself poses, and Paul takes it up next.

Part Six

The Impact

What these texts went on to build.

Trace the impact through its mechanisms, text to institution to behavior, and the first mechanism is a sentence. Within a few years of the crucifixion, the resurrection claim was already being handed on in a fixed form: Christ died for our sins in accordance with the scriptures, he was buried, he was raised on the third day in accordance with the scriptures.

Paul quotes that form in 1 Corinthians 15 and says he received it. The phrase “in accordance with the scriptures” is this session’s texts, compressed into a formula. The claim was scriptural from its first articulation.

The Nicene Creed, three centuries later, still carries the compression: “on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures.” Millions who could not name Daniel or Hosea have recited the footprint of this library. The texts built the grammar in which the claim is still stated.

The second mechanism is preaching. The first Christian sermon Luke preserves, Peter’s in Acts 2, is not a story about an empty tomb. It is an exegesis of Psalm 16 and Psalm 110, argued line by line before a Jerusalem crowd.

Whatever happened at the tomb, the public case was made from these psalms, in the open, with the texts as the evidence. Christianity’s founding argument, as Luke preserves it, is a Bible study.

The third mechanism is boundary-making, and it ran in both traditions. The Mishnah, Judaism’s law code compiled around AD 200, opens its list of those with no share in the world to come with a man who says resurrection is not from the Torah. Daniel’s innovation had become a test of belonging. The idea born under Antiochus’s persecution ended as doctrine on both sides of the parting of the ways, defended by each against the other.

The fourth mechanism is the calendar. Isaiah’s servant is still read aloud on Good Friday in the churches’ Holy Week, and Psalm 22 still opens the worship of the cross’s week, its first line on the lips of congregations who know how it ends. Genesis 22 is read at Rosh Hashanah, and the horn blown there is a ram’s horn, the shofar, the thicket’s echo carried into the liturgy of every new year. These texts are not only argued about. They are scheduled, annually, into the lives of communities, which is how a text outlives every argument about it.

The fifth mechanism is art, and music carries it furthest. Handel’s Messiah, first performed in 1742, sets the servant at the center of its second part: surely he has borne our griefs, and with his stripes we are healed, all we like sheep. For most of its hearers across three centuries, Isaiah 53 has a soundtrack, and it is Handel’s.

Daniel’s Ancient of Days passed into the visual imagination by the same road, down to Blake’s famous print of 1794: the white-haired Ancient crouching in a blaze of glory, compasses in hand, measuring out the darkness. A second-century BC vision became part of the permanent furniture of the Western mind.

And the argument the texts started has never closed. Isaiah 53 remains the most contested chapter in Jewish and Christian reading, each side certain the other is misreading its own book. Origen was already recording that argument, in nearly its modern form, in the third century AD.

The texts behind Mark are not background. They are the ground on which the argument about Jesus has been conducted from the first generation to this one.

The next sessions take up the argument’s most formidable early mind: Paul, whose letters are the oldest Christian writings we have, and whose resurrection chapter is the centerpiece of the case.
