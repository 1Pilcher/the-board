World

Moscow

Putin meeting ends with the trilateral talks restarted and Europe's seat still in dispute

Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, President Trump's envoys, spent Thursday in Moscow with Vladimir Putin, carrying the results of two days of talks in Abu Dhabi between American, Russian and Ukrainian negotiators, the first of their kind since February. Abu Dhabi ended with an agreement in principle on a large exchange of prisoners and no movement on a ceasefire, territory or security guarantees.

Leaving from Vnukovo Airport, Witkoff said: "People didn't think that we were going to restart trilateral. We've restarted it. We've set a new meeting." He said European representatives would join the next round. Putin's aide Yuri Ushakov confirmed the trilateral talks would continue but played down any European role, and the envoys flew to Dublin to brief Zelensky.

The fighting did not pause for the diplomacy. Zelensky said Thursday that Starshield, the military version of SpaceX's Starlink service that Ukraine's drone defenses depend on, has faced significant disruptions and systematic speed restrictions for several days. SpaceX told CNN there had been no change in its service.

Strait of Hormuz

Iran says two supertankers hit mines in the strait; Brent rose 0.7% anyway

Iran's Revolutionary Guards Navy said on Thursday that two oil supertankers were disabled by massive explosions after hitting naval mines on Wednesday night, after ignoring warnings against an unauthorized route through the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint for roughly a fifth of the world's oil supply. The claim has not been independently verified, and tankers in the strait were reported ablaze.

Oil fell more than $1 a barrel in early Asian trading, then reversed: Brent was up 0.7% at $93.40 a barrel by mid-afternoon in London, helped by President Trump's post ruling out a deal with Iran. OPEC+ production rose in October, led by Saudi Arabia, which capped the move, and Baltic Exchange rates for tankers loading outside the strait have fallen about 50% from last month's peaks, a sign the physical market is calmer than the headlines.

US

Mississippi

Colom beats Hyde-Smith by about 4,700 votes, and Mississippi has its first Black senator since Reconstruction

The Associated Press called Mississippi's Senate race for Democrat Scott Colom on Wednesday, more than a week after election day, once the last absentee and provisional ballots were counted. Colom, a 43-year-old district attorney from Columbus, took 49.2% of the vote to Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith's 48.7%, a margin of 4,669 votes out of 962,237 counted. On election night his lead had been 43 votes.

Hyde-Smith telephoned Colom and then conceded in a video, saying she respected the decision of Mississippi's voters. She had history with the man who beat her: in 2023 she used a Senate procedure to block Colom's nomination to a federal judgeship. Colom ran on the cost of living in a state President Trump carried by 23 points in 2024, and he becomes the first Democrat elected to the Senate from Mississippi in more than 40 years.

Brooklyn, New York

City blames unsafe work next door for the Flatbush collapse that killed 8

The three-story building at 1321 Flatbush Avenue, four apartments built in 1930, fell into the open excavation beside it at about 4 p.m. on Wednesday. As of Thursday afternoon, the Fire Department's count was 8 people killed and 19 injured, three of them firefighters, and crews were still searching the rubble.

In a vacate order issued Thursday, the Department of Buildings blamed the collapse on unsafe construction work at the lot next door, 2625 Bedford Avenue. An inspection commissioned by the developers' own contractors and filed in March had flagged cracks in the building's facade. The city ordered demolition on the lot stopped that month; the contractor defied the order and was fined, the stop was lifted in May, and excavation followed.

Houston

Council approves a $2 million Ernst & Young review of the airport system, with a 90-day clock attached

Houston City Council voted on Tuesday to pay Ernst & Young up to $1,995,000 for a system-wide operational, organizational and financial assessment of the Houston Airport System, the city department that runs Bush Intercontinental, Hobby and Ellington airports. The money comes from the Airport System Revenue Fund: $1,695,000 this fiscal year and $300,000 later. The scope covers spending patterns, staffing, permitting fees and cost recovery, bringing contracted services in house, retention incentives, IT and cybersecurity, and airline and non-airline revenue, benchmarked against peer airports. No bids were solicited; the contract runs through a cooperative purchasing agreement.

The condition that matters came from Council Member Julian Ramirez. Within 90 days of the administration accepting any report or deliverable from the firm, it must present the findings to the Budget and Fiscal Affairs Committee. The clock starts on acceptance, and the administration decides when that is. Council Member Edward Pollard had held the item for a week, pointing out that the council is still waiting on results from the firm's earlier city work. The City Controller's auditors counted 211 open audit findings across city government in October; 9 of them belong to the airport system.

Texas · The grid

ERCOT's chief wants authority to order data centers to cut power at the first sign of an emergency

Pablo Vegas, the chief executive of ERCOT, the operator of the Texas electric grid, said on Wednesday at the BloombergNEF Summit in Houston that he will ask the Legislature to let ERCOT order data centers and other large users to reduce power use when the grid reaches the first level of its three-level emergency scale, or earlier. Today the trigger is level two.

A 2025 law, Senate Bill 6, already obliges large users, data centers first, to cut use on 24 hours' notice, but ERCOT has never directed a data center to curtail under it; facilities did cut voluntarily during extreme weather this past winter. The Legislature convenes on January 12, 2027, and grid access for data centers is shaping up as one of its central fights.

Texas · Data centers

A tax break estimated at $14.6 million is now projected to cost $3.3 billion, and lawmakers filed to repeal it on day one

When Texas created its sales tax exemption for large data centers in 2013, the official estimate put the cost at $14.6 million for the 2014-15 budget cycle. The comptroller now projects $3.3 billion for 2028-29. To qualify, a data center needs at least 100,000 square feet, $200 million of investment over five years and 20 jobs paying above the local average; in return it pays none of the state's 6.25% sales tax on servers, cooling equipment and electricity, for up to 15 or 20 years.

Bill filing for the 2027 session opened on Monday, and at least five bills would repeal the exemption, including HB 167 in the House and SB 293 by Senator Joan Huffman of Houston, who chairs the Senate Finance Committee; 23 of the Senate's 31 members or members-elect had signed onto her bill by Monday evening. The bills agree on ending the break for new projects and split on existing ones: the House bill lets certified facilities keep the exemption until their terms expire, while SB 293, as its author describes it, ends theirs when the law takes effect. State audits have reviewed 20 facilities and found 6 that did not meet the law's requirements.

The governor has already paused ERCOT interconnection approvals and state environmental permits for data center projects, pending an audit due in December. About 410 gigawatts of large loads are seeking connection to the Texas grid, 87% of them data centers.

Markets

Nvidia fell 5.5% on the day its founder resigned, after plunging 14.4% at the open

Nvidia announced at 8:48 a.m. ET on Thursday that Jensen Huang, who founded the company in 1993 and ran it for 33 years, had resigned as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately, with chief financial officer Colette Kress serving as interim chief executive. The shares plunged 14.4% at the open, to their lowest intraday level in more than a year, then recovered part of the drop. The wider market barely reacted.

The repricing started before the announcement. In two days, three brokerages stepped back from the stock, all citing who would run the company rather than what it sells. Bernstein halved its price target to $200 from $400 while leaving its earnings forecasts unchanged: it cut the multiple it will pay for Nvidia's earnings from about 25 times to about 13 times, and analyst Stacy Rasgon said the move was about leadership uncertainty, not weak chip sales. Baird cut its target to $200 from $500 and moved to neutral; Morgan Stanley removed Nvidia from its top picks on Wednesday.

Analyst Dan Ives put a price on the founder: $300 a share with Huang, $225 without him, a 25% premium for one man. The company dipped below a $4 trillion market value during the morning. Two things settle the argument from here: a permanent successor, and Nvidia's quarterly results on November 17, when the new leadership faces questions about demand and about who is in charge.

The Gulf

The fastest ocean liner ever built was sunk on purpose Wednesday night, and it is now the world's largest artificial reef

The SS United States was launched in 1951, measures 990 feet, longer than the Titanic, and crossed the Atlantic in under four days, a speed record no passenger liner has beaten. It carried four presidents and, once, the Mona Lisa. Retired in 1969, it spent decades rusting while its owners looked for a future for it.

On Wednesday, Okaloosa County, Florida, sank it on purpose, a day ahead of schedule because of coming weather. Crews opened plugs in the hull and let it flood, no explosives. The ship settled upright at 9:59 p.m. ET, 7 hours and 19 minutes after the flooding began, in about 180 feet of water, 22 nautical miles southwest of Destin. Its upper decks sit 55 to 60 feet below the surface, shallow enough for beginning divers, and it passes the aircraft carrier USS Oriskany, sunk off Pensacola in 2006, as the largest artificial reef in the world.
